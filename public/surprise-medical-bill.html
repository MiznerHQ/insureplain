Surprise Medical Bill:
Where Your Protection Actually Stops

Medical Bills

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

You went to an in-network hospital and did everything right. Then a bill showed up from a doctor you never chose — an anesthesiologist, an ER physician, an assistant surgeon — billed as if you'd walked into their office off the street.

Since January 1, 2022, the federal No Surprises Act blocks most bills like that. But "most" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The law has real, specific edges — a transportation gap, a consent trap providers still try to use, and two completely different dispute processes depending on whether you have insurance. Most explainers stop at "you're protected." This one shows you exactly where that protection ends and what to do when it does.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is my ambulance ride protected?
Only if it's air ambulance. Ground ambulance is excluded from the No Surprises Act entirely — an out-of-network ground crew can still bill you the full balance, no matter how the trip was called in.
Can I be pressured to sign my rights away?
Sometimes legally, sometimes not. A narrow list of specialties — emergency medicine, anesthesiology, radiology, and a few others — can never ask you to waive your protections, regardless of what paperwork they hand you.
Does having insurance change how I fight the bill?
Yes, completely. Insured patients push the dispute through their insurer; uninsured and self-pay patients use a separate government process tied to their Good Faith Estimate.
The boundary line

What the law protects — and what it doesn't

The No Surprises Act protects you in two situations: true emergency care at any facility, and out-of-network providers who treat you at an in-network facility you chose yourself. In both cases, the most you can owe is your normal in-network deductible and copay — the insurer and provider settle the rest between themselves, and you're not part of that fight.

✓ Protected

Emergency care anywhere. Anesthesiologists, radiologists, pathologists, and assistant surgeons at an in-network hospital. Air ambulance from an in-network operator.

✗ Not protected

Ground ambulance. Care at a facility you knowingly chose out-of-network. Non-emergency services where you validly signed away your rights in advance.

The consent-waiver trap

Which specialties can never ask you to waive protection

Some providers still hand patients a form asking them to give up No Surprises Act protection. It's only legal in narrow conditions: the visit must be non-emergency, the provider must give written notice at least 72 hours ahead of the appointment, and you must sign it voluntarily, not under pressure while you're already checked in.

More importantly, an entire category of "ancillary" specialties is banned from ever asking, full stop — even with a form, even with 72 hours' notice, even in a non-emergency:

  • Emergency medicine physicians — waivers are void even outside a technical "emergency" visit.
  • Anesthesiologists — you don't choose who administers anesthesia.
  • Radiologists and pathologists — you never interact with them directly.
  • Neonatologists — newborn care can't carry a waiver.
  • Assistant surgeons, hospitalists, and intensivists — assigned to you by the hospital, not chosen.

⚠️ If you signed something

A waiver from any provider on this list is not enforceable, regardless of what it says or when you signed it. If you're billed after signing one from a banned specialty, that's grounds for a federal complaint, not a valid bill.

Two different fights

Insured vs. uninsured: the dispute path splits

This is the part most guides collapse into one generic "dispute it" instruction — but the actual process depends entirely on your coverage status.

If you're insured

Your insurer, not you, is the one who fights the price. Call and confirm the bill is No Surprises Act-eligible; if it is, they must reprocess it at in-network rates. If your insurer and the provider can't agree, it goes to federal independent dispute resolution — a process between the two of them, not you.

If you're uninsured or self-pay

There's no insurer to fight for you, so the law gives you your own tool: compare the final bill to the Good Faith Estimate you were owed before treatment. If it's more than $400 higher, you can file a patient-provider dispute resolution claim yourself.

⏱ Deadline warning

The uninsured/self-pay dispute path has a hard 120-day window from the date on the bill. Miss it and you generally lose access to this specific process — don't sit on a Good Faith Estimate mismatch.

How to fight back — step by step

Dispute the bill

  1. Don't pay yet. Paying before disputing can be read as acceptance. Hold off until you've reviewed everything below.
  2. Request an itemized bill. Call the provider's billing department and ask for a line-by-line breakdown — it's your legal right, and billing errors are common.
  3. Pull your EOB. If insured, compare your Explanation of Benefits against what the provider billed. Mismatches go to your insurer first.
  4. Confirm coverage under the law. Ask your insurer directly: "Is this covered under the No Surprises Act?" A yes means mandatory reprocessing at in-network rates.
  5. Check if it's actually a different problem. Some bills that look like surprise billing are really a coverage denial in disguise — worth ruling out separately if the insurer's reasoning cites medical necessity rather than network status. See our guide on what "not medically necessary" actually means.
  6. File a federal complaint if your insurer won't act. Report it at federalnosurprises.cms.gov or 1-800-985-3059 — complaints trigger real review.
  7. Uninsured: file patient-provider dispute resolution. If your bill beats your Good Faith Estimate by $400+, start this process within 120 days.
  8. Negotiate what's left. For anything outside the law's protection, ask the billing department directly for a cash-pay discount, payment plan, or charity-care application — most hospitals have one they don't advertise.
What to do right now
Don't pay the bill yet. Figure out which category it falls into first: emergency care or an out-of-network provider at an in-network facility (protected), ground ambulance or a knowingly out-of-network choice (not protected), or a bill higher than your Good Faith Estimate (its own 120-day process). Once you know which lane you're in, the right next call — your insurer, the provider's billing office, or the federal complaint line — becomes obvious. Frequently asked questions
What is the No Surprises Act?
The No Surprises Act is a federal law, effective January 1, 2022, that limits what you can be charged for emergency care and for out-of-network providers who treat you at an in-network facility, like anesthesiologists or radiologists you never personally chose. In protected situations, you can only be billed your normal in-network cost-sharing amount.
Can a doctor ask me to waive my No Surprises Act protections?
Only in narrow circumstances, and only for non-emergency care. The provider must give written notice and consent at least 72 hours before the appointment, and you must sign voluntarily. Emergency physicians, anesthesiologists, radiologists, pathologists, neonatologists, assistant surgeons, hospitalists, and intensivists can never legally ask you to waive your protections, no matter what form they hand you.
Does the No Surprises Act cover ground ambulance bills?
No. Ground ambulance rides are the single biggest gap in the law. An out-of-network ground ambulance can still bill you the full difference between its charge and what your insurer paid, often thousands of dollars. Air ambulance is protected; ground ambulance currently is not.
Does the dispute process differ if I'm insured vs. uninsured?
Yes. If you're insured and the bill is covered by the No Surprises Act, your insurer must reprocess it at in-network rates, and unresolved disputes go through the independent dispute resolution process between your insurer and the provider. If you're uninsured or paying cash, you compare the final bill to your Good Faith Estimate and, if it's more than $400 higher, file a patient-provider dispute resolution claim yourself.
How long do I have to dispute a bill that's higher than my Good Faith Estimate?
120 days from the date on the bill. This deadline applies to the patient-provider dispute resolution process for uninsured and self-pay patients whose final bill exceeds their Good Faith Estimate by more than $400. Miss the window and you generally lose the right to use this specific process.
