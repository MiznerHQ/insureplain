My Health Sharing Ministry
Won't Pay My Bill — Now What?

Health Sharing

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

You joined a health sharing ministry, paid your monthly share on time, submitted a medical bill — and it came back declined, delayed, or only partially covered. Your instinct is to appeal. That's the right instinct with real insurance, and it's exactly where health sharing runs into trouble.

A health sharing ministry is not an insurance policy, so it isn't bound by the appeal rights, external review process, or state insurance commissioner oversight that comes with a regulated plan. That doesn't mean you have zero options — it means your options are different, narrower, and rarely explained anywhere the average member would think to look before joining.

This guide covers what actually happens when a sharing request is declined, the one real complaint route available in many states, and a timing trap tied to membership status changes that catches people off guard more often than the guidelines make obvious. It's a different problem than a regular insurance claim denial, and it needs a different response.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Can I sue a health sharing ministry for not paying?
It's possible, but difficult and rarely worth it for smaller bills. Because membership agreements are voluntary and ministries are exempt from insurance regulation in most states, a lawsuit would typically hinge on contract terms in the guidelines you agreed to, not insurance law.
Does my state regulate health sharing ministries at all?
A handful of states have added light consumer disclosure rules, but most treat ministries as exempt from insurance oversight entirely. Check your state Attorney General's website for any HCSM-specific guidance before assuming there's no oversight at all.
Will a health sharing ministry ever pay a large hospital bill?
Often, yes — many do share large bills that clearly meet their published guidelines. The risk isn't that they never pay; it's that there's no enforceable guarantee, and edge cases like timing or eligibility disputes are where members get stuck.
The core problem

Why there's no formal appeal like real insurance

With a denied insurance claim, you have a legal right to an internal appeal and, in most states, an independent external review — a real regulator can eventually force the issue. Health sharing ministries don't carry that structure, and understanding exactly what replaces it changes how you should respond to a decline.

  • Internal review only — Most ministries offer a review by their own staff or board. It's worth requesting, but the same organization that declined your bill is the one deciding whether to reverse it.
  • No independent external review — Unlike a real insurance appeal, there's no neutral third party required by law to weigh in if the ministry's internal review upholds the denial.
  • Sharing is voluntary, not contractual payment — Guidelines describe what's eligible, but eligibility isn't the same as a guarantee. Members are asked to share, not legally compelled to.
  • Discretion sits with the ministry — Borderline cases, including a partial pre-existing condition overlap or ambiguous eligibility, are typically decided in the ministry's favor unless you push back with documentation.
  • No state insurance commissioner backstop — The regulator who could intervene on a real denied claim generally has no jurisdiction here, because the ministry was never licensed as an insurer.
Your real options

Internal review vs. the one outside complaint route

Two paths exist once a bill is declined — one inside the ministry, one outside it. Knowing both, and their limits, keeps you from wasting time on the wrong one first.

Internal Review

Request it in writing, citing the specific guideline you believe supports your bill. Fast, but the ministry has final say — it isn't a neutral decision-maker.

State Attorney General

In states that accept complaints, filing creates a formal record and sometimes prompts a second look. It doesn't compel payment the way a state insurance department can with a licensed insurer.

⚠️ THE MEMBERSHIP-STATUS TIMING TRAP

Many ministries won't share expenses incurred within a set window — often around 60 days — after a change to your membership status, including switching tiers, pausing coverage, or reinstating after a lapse. If your bill lands near a status change you made, check the exact date rules in your current guidelines before assuming the denial was about the medical claim itself.

When a bill gets declined

What to do, in order

  1. Get the denial reason in writing. Ask exactly which guideline provision the ministry cited — don't accept a vague "not eligible" explanation.
  2. Pull the guidelines version in effect on your service date. Ministries update guidelines periodically; the version that matters is the one active when you received care, not the current one.
  3. Check for a status-change timing conflict. Confirm whether any recent tier switch, pause, or reinstatement falls inside an exclusion window — similar in spirit to how an insurance waiting period works, but set entirely by the ministry's own guidelines.
  4. Request internal review in writing. Cite the specific provision you believe supports your bill and ask for a written response, not a phone call.
  5. File with your state Attorney General's consumer affairs office if internal review fails. Confirm your state accepts HCSM complaints first — not every state does.
  6. Compare your real options going forward. If this keeps happening, price out a regulated plan through marketplace subsidies or short-term health insurance as a fallback with an actual appeal process behind it.
What to do right now
Request your denial reason in writing today, and pull the guidelines version that was active on your service date — not the current one on the website. If a membership status change happened near your service date, check the exclusion window first, since that's the most commonly missed reason for a decline that looks like a coverage dispute. If internal review doesn't resolve it, confirm whether your state Attorney General's office accepts health sharing ministry complaints before deciding your next move.
Frequently asked questions
Can I appeal if my health sharing ministry denies my bill?
Not in the way you can with real insurance. Most ministries offer an internal review by their own staff or board, but there is no independent, external appeal process required by law, because health sharing ministries are not legally insurance.
Is there anywhere I can complain if my health sharing ministry won't pay?
In many states, you can file a complaint with your state Attorney General's consumer affairs office. This won't force payment the way a state insurance commissioner can with a real insurer, but it creates a record and sometimes prompts a second look.
Why did my ministry stop sharing my bill after I changed membership tiers?
Many ministries exclude expenses incurred within a set window after a membership status change, often around 60 days. Switching tiers, pausing, or reinstating your membership can trigger this window without you realizing it applied to your bill.
Is a health sharing ministry legally required to pay anything?
No. Sharing is voluntary, not a binding contractual obligation. Because ministries are exempt from insurance regulation in most states, no law compels them to pay a specific bill even if it appears to meet their published guidelines.
What should I do before I get a large bill, to avoid a denial like this?
Read the current published guidelines in full, confirm how any recent or upcoming membership status change affects timing, and keep a copy of the guidelines version in effect on your service date. That record matters if a dispute comes up later.
