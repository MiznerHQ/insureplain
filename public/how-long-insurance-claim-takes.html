How Long Does an Insurance Claim
Actually Take?

Claims & Payments

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

Most answers to "how long does a claim take" boil down to a shrug: somewhere between a week and a few months, depending. That's not wrong, but it's incomplete. Federal law actually sets specific decision deadlines for health insurance claims — the range isn't a guess, it's a set of legal clocks that start ticking the moment your provider submits the claim, and they're different depending on what kind of claim it is and what kind of plan you have.

The three clocks that matter most: 30 days for a standard claim after you've already received care, 15 days for a claim that needs pre-approval before you get care, and just 72 hours if the claim involves urgent or time-sensitive treatment. Almost nothing you'll find online states these numbers plainly, because most claims content is written for auto insurance, where the rules are entirely different and set by state law instead of federal law.

What actually determines your timeline is your plan type — an employer-sponsored plan governed by ERISA follows one set of rules, a plan you bought yourself on the ACA marketplace follows a mix of federal and state rules, and Medicare or Medicaid follow their own separate schedules entirely. If you're between plans — say, transitioning onto COBRA after a job change — confirm which plan was actually active on the date of service before you start counting days. Knowing which bucket you're in is the difference between waiting patiently and knowing exactly when you're allowed to push back.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is the 30-day window business days or calendar days?
Calendar days, not business days. The federal 30-day post-service claim deadline counts every day on the calendar, including weekends. Some state prompt-pay laws for marketplace plans use business days instead, which is one reason the exact deadline can shift by a few days depending on your state.
Why does my claim still say "pending" after 30 days?
Almost always because of a paused clock, not a broken one. If the insurer sent a written request for more information — a corrected code, additional medical records, proof of another insurance policy — the 30-day countdown stops until that information is received.
Can an insurance company legally take longer than 30 days?
Yes, but only with a valid reason on record. An insurer can extend the standard deadline once, typically by up to 15 additional days, if it notifies you in writing before the original deadline and explains what it's still waiting on.
Does an urgent care claim get decided faster?
Yes, dramatically faster. Claims involving urgent care — where a standard timeline could seriously jeopardize your health — must be decided within 72 hours under federal rules, not 30 days.
The federal claim clock

How long each type of claim is legally allowed to take

These deadlines come from federal claims-procedure rules that apply to most employer and marketplace health plans. If your deductible or plan design changed recently, the claim type below still governs the timeline regardless of what you owe out of pocket.

  • Post-service claims (30 days) — the most common type: you already received care, and the provider billed your insurer afterward. The insurer has 30 calendar days to make a decision.
  • Pre-service claims (15 days) — claims requiring approval before you get care, like a scheduled surgery or imaging that needs prior authorization. The insurer has 15 days to decide.
  • Urgent care claims (72 hours) — any claim where waiting the standard 15 days could seriously risk your health. These must be decided within 72 hours, and can be even faster if a delay would clearly endanger you.
  • Concurrent care claims (24 hours) — a request to extend an ongoing course of treatment, like additional hospital days. If it's urgent, the insurer must respond within 24 hours of receiving the request.
  • One 15-day extension — insurers can extend most deadlines once, by up to 15 days, but only by notifying you in writing before the original deadline expires and stating the specific reason.
  • State prompt-pay overlays — if you're on a marketplace or individually purchased plan, your state may layer additional prompt-pay requirements on top of the federal floor, which can shorten the practical deadline further.
In-network vs. out-of-network

Why the same 30-day rule can feel very different in practice

The legal deadline doesn't change based on network status, but the real-world timeline usually does. What you actually owe once the claim clears depends on your copay vs. coinsurance structure, but that split has no bearing on how long the decision itself takes. Here's why one network status drags longer than the other even under the identical federal rule.

In-network claim

Your provider already has a negotiated rate on file with the insurer and typically files electronically with standardized codes, so the claim usually clears at the faster end of the 30-day window with no information request needed.

Out-of-network claim

There's no pre-set price, so the insurer has to manually determine an allowed amount before it can issue a decision. Out-of-network claims are also more likely to trigger a request for additional records, which pauses the clock.

⚠️ THE "CLOCK PAUSE" MOST PEOPLE MISS

The moment an insurer sends a written request for more information, the 30-day countdown pauses — it does not restart at zero, and it does not keep running in the background. You then generally have 45 days to respond. Once your information is received, the insurer resumes counting whatever decision days it had left, not a fresh 30. Watch your mail and your insurer's online portal closely; a missed information request is the single most common reason a claim appears stalled.

When the deadline passes

What to do if your claim is overdue

  1. Confirm the claim was actually received. Log into your insurer's member portal or call to verify the exact date the claim was received — not the date of service, since a delay in your provider submitting the claim doesn't count against the insurer's clock.
  2. Check for an information request you may have missed. Look for any letter or portal message asking for more documentation. If one exists, the clock is legitimately paused and the delay is on you or your provider, not the insurer.
  3. Get the decision date in writing. If there's no pending information request and the deadline has passed, ask the insurer in writing for the specific reason for the delay and a firm date by which you'll receive a decision.
  4. Treat a non-answer as a denial. If the insurer still won't give you a decision or a date, you can treat the claim as denied for purposes of appeal and begin the process described in our guide on how to appeal an insurance denial — you don't have to wait indefinitely for a formal denial letter.
  5. File a regulatory complaint if needed. For an employer-sponsored plan, that's the U.S. Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration. For a marketplace or individual plan, that's your state's Department of Insurance.
  6. Keep every document. Save the original claim confirmation, any information requests, your call logs with reference numbers, and copies of anything you send back — this record is exactly what a complaint or appeal will ask you to produce.
What to do right now
Pull up your insurer's online portal today and check your claim's exact received date and current status. If it's past 30 days for a standard claim with no information request on file, call and ask directly for the reason and a written decision date — and if you get an EOB that doesn't match what you expected, don't assume it's final until you've confirmed the clock actually ran its course. If this claim is pushing you close to your out-of-pocket maximum, tracking the running total matters even more while you wait on a decision.
Frequently asked questions
Does the 30-day clock restart if the insurer asks for more information?
No, the clock doesn't restart from zero, but it does pause. Once the insurer sends a written request for additional information, the countdown stops until you or your provider supply what was asked for. You then typically have 45 days to respond, and once your response is received, the insurer's remaining decision days pick back up. This is the single most common reason a claim seems to disappear past the 30-day mark — check your mail and your insurer's online portal for a pending information request before assuming the deadline was ignored.
What happens if my claim shows "in process" for months with no update?
A claim that shows no movement for months, with no information request on file, is a compliance problem, not a normal delay. At that point you have the right to ask your insurer in writing for the specific reason for the delay and a decision date, and if you don't get a clear answer, you can file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Labor for an employer plan or your state Department of Insurance for a marketplace or individual plan.
Are out-of-network claim timelines different from in-network?
The legal deadline is the same either way, but out-of-network claims usually take longer in practice because there's no negotiated rate on file, so the insurer has to manually price the service before it can issue a decision. Out-of-network claims are also more likely to trigger an information request, since providers outside the network don't always submit standardized coding the way in-network providers do.
If the insurer misses its own deadline, does my claim get auto-approved?
Usually not automatically, though a small number of states do have prompt-pay laws that treat a claim as approved by default if the insurer blows the deadline without requesting an extension. In most cases, a missed deadline is treated as a denial you have the right to appeal immediately, and it also becomes grounds for a formal complaint about how the claim was handled, separate from the appeal itself.
Does calling to check my claim status count as anything, or just wait?
Calling won't speed up the internal review, but it does create a record, which matters if you need to escalate later. Ask for the exact date the claim was received, whether any information request was ever sent, and get a reference or call log number for that conversation — that paper trail is exactly what a state insurance complaint or appeal will ask you to provide.
