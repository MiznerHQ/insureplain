Copay vs Coinsurance:
What's the Real Difference?

Health Insurance Basics

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated August 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

You're looking at a medical bill or an insurance card, and two terms keep showing up: copay and coinsurance. They both mean "money you owe," but they work in completely different ways — and mixing them up can leave you badly surprised by a bill.

A copay is a flat, fixed dollar amount you pay for a specific type of visit or service — say, $30 every time you see your primary doctor, no matter what happens during that visit. Coinsurance is different: it's a percentage of the total cost of a service, so the dollar amount changes depending on how expensive that service turns out to be.

Most health plans use both. Copays tend to apply to routine, predictable visits. Coinsurance tends to kick in for bigger, less predictable costs like surgery, hospital stays, or specialist procedures — which is exactly why it can catch people off guard.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Which one is more common — copay or coinsurance?
Most plans use both, applied to different situations. Copays typically cover routine visits like check-ups or urgent care, while coinsurance applies to bigger services like surgery, imaging, or specialist care.
Can my plan have only one and not the other?
Yes, some plans do. High-deductible plans in particular sometimes skip copays entirely and use coinsurance for everything once you've met your deductible.
Does either one apply before I've met my deductible?
It depends on your plan. Some plans let you pay just a copay for certain visits even before your deductible is met, while coinsurance almost always waits until after the deductible.
How copays work

The flat-fee side of your plan

A copay is designed to be predictable. You know the number before you walk in the door, and it doesn't change based on what the doctor actually does during your visit. Common copay situations include:

  • Primary care visit — often $20–$40, shown right on your insurance card.
  • Specialist visit — usually higher than primary care, often $40–$75.
  • Urgent care — typically $50–$100 depending on the plan.
  • Emergency room — can be $150–$500+, sometimes waived if you're admitted to the hospital.
  • Prescription drugs — copays are often tiered, with generic drugs cheapest and specialty drugs most expensive. See how this compares with an HSA or FSA if you're managing prescription costs.
  • Mental health visits — frequently matched to the primary care copay under current parity laws.
How coinsurance works

The percentage side of your plan

Coinsurance is where costs get less predictable, because it's calculated as a percentage of whatever the service actually costs — and that number can vary a lot from provider to provider, and from visit to visit.

Copay example

You see a specialist. Your plan says $50 copay. You pay $50 flat, regardless of what tests or treatment happen during the visit.

Coinsurance example

You have outpatient surgery billed at $10,000. Your plan is 80/20 coinsurance. You owe 20% — $2,000 — once your deductible is met.

⚠️ Coinsurance can add up fast

Because coinsurance scales with the total bill, a single expensive procedure can cost you thousands more than a copay ever would. This is exactly why your out-of-pocket maximum exists — it's the ceiling that caps how much coinsurance can cost you in a single plan year.

How to check your bill

Figuring out which one you're actually paying

  1. Check your insurance card first. Copay amounts for common visit types are often printed right on the card.
  2. Look at your Summary of Benefits. This document, provided by your insurer, spells out both your copay and coinsurance rates by service type.
  3. Confirm whether the deductible applies. Some copays apply before the deductible; coinsurance almost never does. Review your deductible status for the year.
  4. Read your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) after the visit. This document from your insurer breaks down exactly what was billed, what insurance covered, and what portion was copay vs. coinsurance.
  5. Check whether you're in-network. Coinsurance rates are typically much higher — sometimes dramatically so — if the provider is out-of-network.
  6. Track your running total toward your out-of-pocket max. Once you hit it, both copays and coinsurance typically stop for the rest of the plan year.
What to do right now
Pull up your plan's Summary of Benefits and note the copay and coinsurance amounts for the services you use most — primary care, specialists, and any ongoing prescriptions. If you're choosing between plans during open enrollment, compare both numbers together, not just one, since a plan with a low copay can still have expensive coinsurance for anything beyond routine care.
Frequently asked questions
Is a copay or coinsurance cheaper?
It depends on the service. Copays are usually cheaper for routine, low-cost visits like a check-up. Coinsurance can end up more expensive for big-ticket services like surgery or a hospital stay, since it's a percentage of a much larger bill.
Do copays count toward my deductible?
Usually not. Most plans treat copays as a separate, flat fee that doesn't reduce your deductible balance. Check your plan's Summary of Benefits to confirm, since this varies by insurer.
Do I pay a copay and coinsurance for the same visit?
Sometimes. A single visit can involve both — for example, a flat copay for the office visit itself, plus coinsurance on a procedure or lab work done during that same visit.
Why did my coinsurance amount change between visits?
Coinsurance is a percentage, so it changes based on what the actual service costs. A cheaper visit means a smaller coinsurance amount; a more expensive one means a larger amount, even at the same percentage rate.
Does coinsurance stop once I hit my out-of-pocket maximum?
Yes. Once your total out-of-pocket spending for the year reaches your plan's out-of-pocket maximum, your insurer typically covers 100% of covered services for the rest of the plan year, including coinsurance.
