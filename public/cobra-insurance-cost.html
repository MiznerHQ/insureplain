How Much Does COBRA
Insurance Actually Cost?

Coverage Transitions

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

You lost or left a job, and somewhere in the paperwork was a COBRA election notice with a monthly premium that made you do a double take. The number can look like a mistake. It isn't.

COBRA doesn't give you a new, cheaper plan — it lets you keep the exact same employer plan you already had, with the exact same coverage. The catch is that your employer was quietly covering a real chunk of that premium the whole time you worked there, and COBRA removes that subsidy entirely.

So the "sticker shock" isn't COBRA being overpriced. It's you seeing, for the first time, what your health coverage actually costs without an employer absorbing most of it — and for many people, that number lands somewhere between $400 and $700 a month for individual coverage, or well over $1,000 for a family, depending on the plan.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is COBRA the same insurance I had before?
Yes, exactly the same plan. Same network, same doctors, same drug formulary, same deductible progress. COBRA is a continuation of your existing coverage, not a new policy.
How long can I stay on COBRA?
Typically up to 18 months after a job loss or reduction in hours, though certain qualifying events like disability can extend that to 29 or 36 months. Check your election notice for your specific timeline.
Does COBRA cover pre-existing conditions?
Yes, fully. Since it's a continuation of your existing plan rather than new coverage, there's no new waiting period or exclusion for conditions you already had.
Can I switch from COBRA to a marketplace plan later?
Yes. Losing job-based coverage, including the end of COBRA, opens a special enrollment period, so you're not locked into COBRA for its full duration if a marketplace plan becomes the better deal.
Does COBRA cost the same for everyone on the same plan?
Yes, within your employer's group. COBRA premiums are based on the full group rate for the plan tier you were enrolled in, not your individual health status or claims history, so two people on the same plan pay the same COBRA rate.
The real math

What actually drives the COBRA price tag

The number on your notice isn't arbitrary — it's built from a handful of specific factors, most of which trace back to the coverage you already had.

  • Full premium shift — you now pay the entire premium your employer and you split before, which is the single biggest driver of the jump in cost you're seeing.
  • 2% administrative fee — federal law allows plans to add up to 2% on top of the full premium to cover COBRA administration.
  • The plan tier you were already on — a richer plan with a lower deductible costs more to continue than a leaner high-deductible plan would.
  • Family vs. individual coverage — continuing coverage for dependents multiplies the premium, since COBRA preserves whatever tier of coverage you had at the time you left.
  • State mini-COBRA rules — if you're on a state continuation law rather than federal COBRA, pricing structure and maximum duration can differ.
  • Employer severance subsidies — occasionally an employer will pay some or all of your COBRA premium for a set period as part of a severance package, though this isn't required by law.
  • Your W-2's Box 12, Code DD — if you want a quick estimate before your official notice arrives, this box on your last W-2 shows the total annual cost of your employer-sponsored coverage; dividing it by 12 gives a rough monthly COBRA baseline.
Weighing it against alternatives

COBRA vs. a marketplace plan

Before paying the full COBRA premium, it's worth comparing it honestly against a marketplace option, since the right answer depends heavily on your situation.

COBRA makes sense when

You're mid-year on a deductible you don't want to reset, your current doctors are out-of-network on marketplace options, or your former employer is subsidizing part of the premium.

Marketplace usually wins when

You qualify for a subsidy based on your new lower income, since subsidized marketplace premiums are often significantly cheaper than unsubsidized COBRA.

⚠️ You have 60 days to decide

You don't have to elect COBRA immediately. You have 60 days from your coverage-loss date, and electing later still covers you retroactively — so it's worth comparing options rather than signing on day one out of panic.

Take action

What to do when you're offered COBRA

  1. Don't panic-sign immediately. You have 60 days to elect, and coverage is retroactive, so take the time to actually compare your options first.
  2. Check for a special enrollment period. Losing job-based coverage almost always qualifies you for one, opening the door to marketplace subsidies you may not have had access to before.
  3. Ask HR about severance-paid COBRA. Some severance packages include a set number of employer-paid months — always confirm before assuming you're paying the full rate.
  4. Check your state's mini-COBRA rules if your employer has fewer than 20 employees, since federal COBRA may not apply to you at all.
  5. Compare the real out-of-pocket math, not just the premium — factor in your out-of-pocket maximum and deductible progress on your current plan before switching.
  6. Set a payment reminder immediately once you elect, since missing the grace period can end coverage permanently outside a new qualifying event.
What to do right now
If you just received a COBRA notice, don't sign anything on the spot. Pull up a marketplace quote for comparison first — losing job coverage almost always opens a special enrollment window, and depending on your new income, a subsidized plan could easily beat the full COBRA premium. You have 60 days, so there's no reason to decide today.
Frequently asked questions
Can I get COBRA retroactively if I need care before deciding?
Yes. You have 60 days to elect COBRA, and coverage applies retroactively to your last day of employer coverage. If you need care during that decision window, you can elect COBRA afterward and it will cover care received during the gap, as long as you pay the retroactive premiums.
Does my former employer have to subsidize any part of the cost?
No. Under standard COBRA rules, you pay 100% of the premium yourself, plus up to a 2% administrative fee, since your employer's previous subsidy stops once you leave the job. Some severance packages include employer-paid COBRA for a set period, but that's a negotiated benefit, not a legal requirement.
What happens if I miss a COBRA payment deadline?
You typically get a 30-day grace period after each due date before coverage is terminated. Miss that grace period and your coverage is cancelled retroactively to the last paid date, and you generally can't reinstate it outside a new qualifying event.
Is COBRA ever actually cheaper than a marketplace plan?
Occasionally — especially if you're partway through a high deductible year and want to preserve that progress, or if your employer plan offers coverage a marketplace plan wouldn't match. For most people, though, a subsidized marketplace plan costs less.
What's "mini-COBRA" and does my state have it?
Mini-COBRA is a state-level law extending COBRA-style continuation rights to employees of small companies, usually under 20 employees, that federal COBRA doesn't cover. Availability and rules vary by state, so check with your state's department of insurance.
