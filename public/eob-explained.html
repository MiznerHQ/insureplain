Your Explanation of Benefits
Decoded, Line by Line

What's actually happening

A few days or weeks after you see a doctor, an envelope shows up from your insurance company that looks a lot like a bill. It has your name, a dollar amount, and a bunch of codes you don't recognize. It's called an Explanation of Benefits, or EOB, and the very first thing to know is that it is not a bill. Nobody is asking you to pay the number printed on it.

What makes EOBs stressful isn't the document itself — it's that the numbers on it almost never match the bill you get separately from your doctor's office. One says you owe $340. The other says $612. Neither one is lying, but nobody explains why they're different, so most people either overpay out of panic or ignore both and hope it sorts itself out.

An EOB is your insurance company's paper trail of exactly how a claim was priced, discounted, and split between you and your plan. Once you know which line to look at, the mismatch usually has a simple explanation — and this guide walks through it with real numbers instead of vague reassurance.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is an EOB a bill I have to pay?
No. An EOB is a summary from your insurer explaining how a claim was processed. Any amount you actually owe will come as a separate bill from your provider, and it should eventually match what your EOB lists as your responsibility.
How long after my visit will I get an EOB?
Usually 2 to 6 weeks. Timing depends on how quickly your provider submits the claim and how backed up your insurer's processing is. Electronic claims tend to generate an EOB faster than paper ones.
What if my EOB shows $0 owed but I know I paid a copay?
That's normal. A $0 balance on the EOB usually means the visit was fully covered after your copay, or the copay was collected at check-in and isn't reflected as a separate line. It doesn't mean the visit was free.
Can I just ignore my EOB?
Not safely. EOBs are the fastest way to catch billing errors, duplicate charges, or a denied claim before it turns into a collections issue. Skimming each one takes under a minute and can save you real money.
How to read it

Six lines, decoded

Every EOB uses different formatting, but the same six pieces of information show up on almost all of them. Find these on your own EOB and the whole document stops being confusing.

  • Billed amount — what your provider charged before any insurance discount. This number is almost always the highest one on the page, and it is not what you owe.
  • Allowed amount — the discounted rate your insurer negotiated with that provider. This is the real starting point for your costs, and it connects directly to your deductible if you haven't met it yet.
  • Plan paid — the dollar amount your insurance company actually sent to the provider.
  • Your responsibility — what's left after the plan's payment, split between copay, coinsurance, or deductible depending on your plan's structure. See copay vs. coinsurance if those terms blur together.
  • Remark or adjustment codes — short codes explaining any reduction, denial, or special handling on a line item. These are the most skipped-over part of the page and the most useful.
  • Applied to deductible / out-of-pocket total — a running total showing how close you are to your out-of-pocket maximum for the year.
Don't mix these up

EOB vs. bill vs. MSN

Three documents look similar and get lumped together constantly, but only one of them is actually asking for money.

EOB vs. bill

The EOB comes from your insurer and explains the claim. The bill comes from your provider and requests payment. They describe the same visit but serve completely different purposes — pay the bill, not the EOB.

EOB vs. MSN

If you're on Original Medicare, you get a Medicare Summary Notice (MSN) instead of a standard EOB. It covers a whole quarter of claims at once rather than one visit, but functions the same way.

⚠️ If a line item is marked denied

Check the remark code next to it — that's the reason. If you disagree with the reason, most plans give you 180 days from the EOB date to file an appeal. See why claims get denied and how to appeal for the next steps.

When the numbers don't match

What to do, in order

  1. Line up the EOB and the bill side by side. Compare the "your responsibility" line on the EOB directly against the amount due on the bill.
  2. If the bill is higher, wait a few days. Providers sometimes print bills before the claim finishes processing, so the bill can lag behind the EOB's real numbers.
  3. Call the provider's billing office, not your insurer, first. Read them the allowed amount and your responsibility from the EOB and ask them to correct the bill.
  4. If a remark code is unclear, call your insurer's member services line. The number is printed directly on the EOB, and the code explains exactly what happened to that line item.
  5. If you have secondary insurance, wait for both EOBs. Your real balance usually isn't accurate until the secondary plan has also processed the claim.
  6. If you never received an EOB, check your insurer's online portal first. Most carriers default to electronic delivery now, so a "missing" EOB is often just sitting in your member account.
What to do right now
Pull up your most recent EOB and find the "your responsibility" line — that's the only number that matters for what you'll actually owe. Match it against your provider's bill before you pay anything, and if they don't line up, call the provider's billing office first with the EOB's numbers in hand.
Frequently asked questions
My EOB says I owe a different amount than the bill from my doctor — which one is right?
Neither one is automatically right, but the EOB is usually more current. Bills are sometimes printed before the insurer finishes processing the claim, so they can lag behind the real numbers. Call your provider's billing office, read them the allowed amount and your responsibility from the EOB, and ask them to reissue a corrected bill that matches it.
I got a bill before I got an EOB — do I have to pay it now?
Not yet. Insurers generally recommend waiting for the EOB before paying, since the amount on an early bill often reflects the full billed charge rather than what you actually owe after your plan's discount. Most providers won't penalize you for a short delay while you wait for the EOB to arrive.
What does it mean if my EOB shows a service as denied but I never got a denial letter?
The EOB itself often serves as your official denial notice — insurers aren't always required to send a separate letter. Look for a remark code or short reason next to the denied line item; that code explains why, and it's also your starting point if you want to appeal.
I have two insurance plans — why did I get two EOBs for the same visit?
When you have primary and secondary coverage, each insurer processes the claim separately and sends its own EOB. The primary plan's EOB shows what it paid; the secondary plan's EOB shows what it covered from whatever the primary plan left over. Your actual balance is usually only accurate once both have processed.
I never received an EOB at all — is that normal?
It can happen if the claim is still processing, if your insurer switched to electronic-only delivery and it's sitting in your member portal, or if your coverage ended before the claim was filed. Log into your insurer's portal first, since many plans stopped mailing paper EOBs by default.
