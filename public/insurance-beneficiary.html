What Is a Life Insurance
Beneficiary?

Life Insurance

What's actually happening

Most people fill out the beneficiary section of a life insurance application once, file the paperwork away, and never think about it again. That single form ends up carrying more legal weight than almost anything else you'll sign — it decides, by itself, who gets paid when you die, no matter what any other document says.

The problem is that the designation only works the way you assume it does in the simple case: one adult beneficiary, still alive, still the person you'd choose today. Once real life gets more complicated — a minor child, a divorce, an estranged relative you never removed — the form can quietly stop doing what you think it's doing, and most people don't find out until a claim is already being filed.

This guide covers what a beneficiary designation legally is, and the specific situations where it fails silently.

Quick answers

What people ask most

WHAT IS A BENEFICIARY IN LIFE INSURANCE?
A beneficiary is the person or entity named to receive your policy's death benefit. You choose them when you apply for coverage, and you can typically update the designation at any time while the policy is active.
WHAT'S THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A PRIMARY AND CONTINGENT BENEFICIARY?
A primary beneficiary is first in line to receive the payout; a contingent beneficiary only receives it if the primary is unavailable. "Unavailable" usually means they passed away before you, can't be located, or were legally disqualified.
CAN I NAME A TRUST AS MY BENEFICIARY?
Yes, and it's often the cleanest way to control how funds are distributed — especially if a minor child, a dependent who also relies on long-term care coverage, or a blended family is involved. The trust receives the payout directly and distributes it according to terms you set in advance.
Where designations quietly stop working

Three ways your beneficiary form can fail you

Every insurer accepts the form as filled out, even when it's set up in a way that won't actually work when a claim is filed. These are the three most common traps, and none of them show up as an error until someone is trying to collect.

  • Minors can't be paid directly. You can legally name a minor child as beneficiary and the insurer will accept the form — but insurers cannot cut a check directly to someone under 18. Without a trust or UTMA custodian named in advance, the payout goes into a court-supervised guardianship account, which is slower, costs money in legal fees, and hands a court oversight of funds you intended for your child.
  • The designation beats your will, every time. Life insurance proceeds are a direct contract between you and the insurer, not a probate asset — so the policy pays whoever is named on it, regardless of what your will says. A notarized, recently updated will has no power to override an outdated beneficiary form.
  • Divorce doesn't automatically remove an ex. Whether an ex-spouse is automatically dropped as beneficiary after divorce depends on your state and, for employer-provided group policies, on federal law that can override state rules entirely. That distinction is detailed in our guide to life insurance after divorce — worth reading if you've been through one and haven't touched your designation since.
  • A beneficiary who predeceases you with no backup named. If your only primary beneficiary dies before you and you never named a contingent beneficiary, the policy is treated as if you named no one — the payout defaults to your estate.
  • Naming a trust for more control. If you're already using an insurance rider to customize your coverage, it's worth applying the same intentionality to your beneficiary designation — a trust can hold the funds and release them on your terms instead of a lump sum.
Structure and disqualification

Revocable vs. irrevocable — and when a beneficiary loses the right to collect

Beyond primary and contingent, every beneficiary designation also carries a control level that determines whether you can change your mind later.

Revocable beneficiary

The default for most policies. You can change this beneficiary anytime, without their knowledge or consent, simply by submitting a new form.

Irrevocable beneficiary

Must approve any change to the designation, and often any change to the policy itself. This is uncommon and usually tied to a legal agreement, such as a divorce settlement or child support order.

⚠️ SLAYER STATUTE — A TRAP COMPETITORS DON'T MENTION

Every state has some version of a slayer statute: a law that automatically disqualifies a beneficiary who is found to have intentionally caused the insured's death, regardless of what the policy paperwork says. The disqualified beneficiary is treated as if they predeceased you, and the payout passes to the next eligible beneficiary in line — or to your estate if none exists.

Changing your beneficiary

Step by step

  1. Log into your policy's online portal if your insurer offers one — many allow beneficiary changes without any paperwork.
  2. Request a beneficiary change form from your agent or insurer if online access isn't available.
  3. Decide primary vs. contingent, and whether a trust makes more sense than an individual for a minor or dependent with special needs.
  4. Fill out the form completely — full legal names, dates of birth, and relationship to you are typically required, along with contingent beneficiary details.
  5. Submit and confirm in writing — request written confirmation from the insurer that the change was processed and is on file, not just a verbal confirmation over the phone.
  6. Review after every major life event — marriage, divorce, a new child, or a death in the family are all good triggers to double-check your designation, along with any change to your term or whole life policy itself.
  7. Check what happens if you lapse coverage — a beneficiary designation only matters while the policy is active, so if you're behind on premiums, see what happens if you stop paying before assuming the designation still applies.
What to do right now
Pull up your current policy documents and confirm exactly who is listed as primary and contingent beneficiary today — not who you think is listed. If a minor is named with no trust or custodian behind them, if your only primary beneficiary has passed away, or if you've been through a divorce since the form was last updated, contact your insurer directly. Updating a designation is usually a quick form, not a full underwriting process, and it's free.
Frequently asked questions
Can I name my minor child as my life insurance beneficiary?
Insurers will let you list a minor child as beneficiary on the form, but they cannot legally pay a death benefit directly to someone under 18. If a minor is still named when a claim is filed and no trust or custodian is in place, the funds typically go into a court-supervised guardianship account until the child turns 18, which is slower and more expensive than naming a trust or UTMA custodian in advance.
Does a beneficiary designation override my will?
Yes. Life insurance proceeds are a contract between you and the insurer, not a probate asset, so the person named on the policy is paid directly regardless of what your will says. An outdated designation from an ex-spouse or estranged relative can legally override even a notarized, recently updated will.
Can a beneficiary be disqualified for causing my death?
Yes. Every state has some version of a slayer statute that automatically disqualifies a beneficiary who is found to have intentionally caused the insured's death, regardless of what the policy says. In that case the payout is treated as if that beneficiary predeceased you, and it passes to the next eligible beneficiary or contingent beneficiary in line.
What happens if my named beneficiary predeceases me and I never updated it?
If your only primary beneficiary has died and you named a contingent beneficiary, the payout passes to them. If you named no contingent beneficiary, most insurers treat the policy as if you named no one at all, and the death benefit goes to your estate and typically enters probate.
What happens if I don't name a beneficiary on my life insurance policy?
If no beneficiary is named, or all named beneficiaries have passed away, the death benefit typically goes to your estate instead. That usually means the payout goes through probate, which can delay distribution and expose the funds to creditors or estate taxes depending on your state.
