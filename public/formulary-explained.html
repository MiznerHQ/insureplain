What Is a Drug Formulary,
And What Happens When Your Drug Isn't On It

PRESCRIPTION DRUGS

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

A formulary is the list of prescription drugs your health plan has agreed to cover, sorted into pricing tiers. Every plan has one, and every plan's list is different — a drug covered cheaply on one plan can be excluded entirely on another, even from the same insurer.

Most explainers stop at that definition. They'll tell you formularies exist, tiers exist, and that you can "ask for an exception" if your drug isn't covered — then leave you to figure out the rest at the pharmacy counter, prescription in hand, with no idea what happens next or how long it takes.

There are two numbers and one distinction that actually matter here: the federal decision clock your insurer must follow once you file an exception, and the difference between the three separate requests people lump together as "asking for an exception." Knowing which one you need — and how long you're allowed to wait — is the difference between a five-minute pharmacy fix and a month of paying full price.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Can my pharmacist tell me if a drug is on my formulary before I try to fill it?
Usually, yes. Pharmacists can run a test claim to see if a drug is covered and what tier it falls under, though they can't file an exception on your behalf — that has to come from your prescriber.
Does every insurance plan use the same formulary?
No. Formularies are set individually by each plan, even plans sold by the same insurer. A drug covered under one employer's plan may be excluded under a different plan from the identical company.
Will my formulary change during the year I'm already enrolled?
It can. Insurers review formularies periodically and may add or remove drugs mid-year, though most plans are required to notify you in advance if a drug you're actively taking is being dropped.
The tier structure

What each formulary tier actually costs

Formularies group covered drugs into tiers, and your plan's premium has nothing to do with which tier a given drug lands in — that's set by the plan's pharmacy benefit manager, separately, based on cost and availability of alternatives.

  • Tier 1 — Preferred generic — the cheapest tier, typically a flat copay in the $0–$15 range.
  • Tier 2 — Generic — non-preferred generics, usually $15–$35 per fill.
  • Tier 3 — Preferred brand — brand-name drugs the plan favors, often $35–$70 or a percentage coinsurance.
  • Tier 4 — Non-preferred brand — brand-name drugs with a preferred alternative available, frequently 30–50% coinsurance.
  • Tier 5 — Specialty — high-cost biologics and specialty drugs, often 25–33% coinsurance with no cap, sometimes running into the hundreds monthly even after coverage.
  • Not listed at all — the drug isn't on the formulary in any tier, meaning full retail price unless an exception is granted.

Whatever tier your drug lands in will show up on your explanation of benefits after each fill, which is the easiest way to confirm you're actually being charged the tier you expect.

The three paths, explained separately

Tiering exception vs. formulary exception vs. prior authorization

These get blurred into one vague "ask for an exception" step almost everywhere. They're not the same request, and filing the wrong one wastes the clock below — much like confusing in-network and out-of-network rules can quietly double a bill for reasons that have nothing to do with the drug itself.

Tiering exception

Your drug IS covered, but at an expensive tier. You're asking the plan to charge the lower copay of a cheaper tier because the preferred alternatives don't work for you.

Formulary exception

Your drug is NOT on the list at all. You're asking the plan to cover it anyway, usually because every formulary alternative is unsafe or ineffective for you.

A prior authorization is a separate, third process — a review some drugs require before they're covered even if they're already on the formulary at a normal tier. You can read the full mechanics of that fight in our prior authorization guide, since it follows its own approval criteria distinct from formulary status.

⚠️ THE FEDERAL DECISION CLOCK

Once your prescriber submits a supporting statement, your plan must decide a standard exception request within 72 hours. If your doctor marks the request expedited because waiting could seriously harm your health, the plan must decide within 24 hours. This timeline applies to both tiering and formulary exceptions.

How to actually file one

Requesting a formulary or tiering exception

  1. Confirm the tier or absence first. Call your plan or have your pharmacist run a test claim so you know exactly which of the three paths above you need.
  2. Get your prescriber to write the supporting statement. This must explain, in clinical terms, why the formulary alternatives are ineffective, contraindicated, or unsafe for you specifically — a vague "patient prefers this drug" note will not qualify.
  3. Decide if it's urgent. If waiting for the standard timeline could seriously harm your health, say so explicitly and ask your prescriber to mark the request expedited — plans default to the slower 72-hour clock unless you ask.
  4. Submit through your plan's process. Most plans have a specific coverage determination form; using it (rather than a generic letter) avoids processing delays.
  5. Track the clock. Note the date your prescriber's statement was received by the plan — that's when the 72-hour or 24-hour window starts, not the date you first called.
  6. If denied, appeal immediately. You can file an internal appeal and, if that fails, an independent external review — see our appeal guide for the exact letter structure and evidence that moves a denial.
What to do right now
If a drug was rejected at the pharmacy counter, don't pay full price yet — call your plan, find out which of the three paths applies, and have your prescriber submit the supporting statement the same day. Ask explicitly whether your situation qualifies for expedited review; the 24-hour clock only starts if someone asks for it. If cost — not coverage — is the real obstacle, it's also worth checking whether a subsidy adjustment or a different plan tier at renewal would solve the problem for good.
Frequently asked questions
What is a formulary in health insurance?
A formulary is the list of prescription drugs your health plan agrees to cover, organized into cost tiers. Drugs on the list are covered at your plan's normal cost-sharing rate; drugs left off it may not be covered at all unless you request an exception.
What happens if my medication isn't on the formulary?
You can pay full price out of pocket, ask your doctor about a formulary alternative that is covered, or have your prescriber file a formulary exception request asking the plan to cover the drug anyway on medical necessity grounds.
What's the difference between a tiering exception and a formulary exception?
A tiering exception asks the plan to charge you the lower copay of a cheaper tier for a drug that's already covered. A formulary exception asks the plan to cover a drug that isn't on the list at all. They're filed differently and reviewed under the same timelines but are not interchangeable.
How long does a formulary exception take to get approved?
Under federal rules, standard formulary exception requests must get a decision within 72 hours of the plan receiving your prescriber's supporting statement. Expedited requests, where waiting could seriously harm your health, must be decided within 24 hours.
What if my formulary exception is denied?
You can file an internal appeal, and if that's denied too, request an independent external review by a third party not affiliated with your insurer. Many plans allow you to request internal and external review at the same time when the request is expedited.
