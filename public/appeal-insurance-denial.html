How to Appeal
an Insurance Denial

CLAIMS & DENIALS

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

Getting a denial letter feels final, but it almost never is. Every health insurance plan is legally required to give you a way to challenge a denied claim, and that process is called an appeal. It exists specifically because insurers get decisions wrong — coding errors, missing paperwork, and overly narrow "not medically necessary" calls are common, and appeals catch a meaningful share of them.

An appeal is different from simply understanding why you were denied. If you haven't yet worked out the reason code on your denial letter, start with why did insurance deny my claim first — this article picks up from there and focuses purely on what to actually do about it.

There are two stages to know: an internal appeal handled by your own insurer, and an external review handled by an independent third party if the internal appeal doesn't go your way. Both have real deadlines, and missing them can close off your options entirely.

Quick answers

What people ask most

How long do I have to appeal an insurance denial?
Typically 180 days from the denial notice for the internal appeal. Your specific deadline is printed on the denial letter itself, so treat that date as the one that matters, not a general rule of thumb.
Do I need a lawyer to appeal a denial?
Usually not for a first-level internal appeal. Most people handle that stage themselves with their denial letter, medical records, and a doctor's support letter.
What's the difference between an internal and external appeal?
Internal is reviewed by your insurer; external is reviewed by an outside party. The external reviewer's decision is generally binding on the insurance company.
Stage one

The internal appeal

This is your first move, and it happens entirely within your insurance company. A different reviewer than the one who issued the original denial looks at your case fresh. Your denial letter should already reference your deductible status and how the claim was processed, which is useful context to include when you write your appeal.

  • Read the denial letter closely — the specific reason code determines what evidence you need to submit.
  • Gather your medical records — request them directly from your provider's office if you don't already have copies.
  • Get a letter from your doctor — a short statement supporting medical necessity carries real weight.
  • Check your plan's exact appeal window — it's usually 180 days, but confirm the number on your letter.
  • Submit in writing — even if you can call, follow up with a written appeal so there's a paper trail.
  • Keep copies of everything — dates, names, and reference numbers matter if you need to escalate later.
Stage two

Internal vs. external review

If the internal appeal upholds the denial, you're not out of options. Most plans allow you to request an external review by an independent organization with no financial tie to your insurer.

INTERNAL APPEAL

Reviewed by your insurance company. Faster, usually decided within 30-60 days. Free to file.

EXTERNAL REVIEW

Reviewed by an independent third party outside your insurer. Decision is typically binding on the plan.

⚠️ DEADLINES DON'T PAUSE THEMSELVES

External review requests usually must be filed within 4 months of the internal appeal's final denial. If you're also tracking premium costs or reconsidering your plan for next year, a missed appeal window can compound with a missed open enrollment deadline in the same season — don't let both slip.

Writing it

How to write an effective appeal letter

  1. Open with the basics. Your name, policy number, claim number, and the date of the denial letter, right at the top.
  2. State what you're appealing. One sentence: quote the denial reason directly from the letter.
  3. Explain why the denial was wrong. Reference your plan documents, medical necessity, or a processing error — be specific.
  4. Attach supporting evidence. Medical records, your doctor's letter, and any relevant billing detail like your copay or coinsurance breakdown if a cost-sharing error is part of the issue.
  5. Ask for a specific outcome. State plainly that you're requesting the claim be approved and reprocessed.
  6. Send it trackable. Certified mail or the insurer's online portal with a confirmation receipt, never just a phone call alone.
What to do right now
Pull your denial letter and find the exact appeal deadline printed on it — that date overrides any general timeline. Then request your medical records from your provider today, since those take the longest to arrive and you'll need them regardless of which stage you're appealing at.
Frequently asked questions
What if my appeal deadline already passed?
Call your insurer's member services line right away and ask about your options. Some plans allow a good-cause exception for missed deadlines, especially if you were hospitalized or never received the denial notice. If the internal deadline is truly closed, ask whether you can still request an external review, since those timelines sometimes run separately.
Do I need a lawyer to appeal a denial?
Most people appeal successfully without a lawyer, especially for a first-level internal appeal. Consider legal help if the denial involves a large dollar amount, a life-threatening condition, or if your internal appeal is denied and you're heading into external review.
What's the difference between an internal and external appeal?
An internal appeal is reviewed by your own insurance company, usually by someone who wasn't involved in the original denial. An external review is decided by an independent third party outside your insurer, and that decision is typically binding on the insurance company.
Can I appeal a denial more than once?
Most plans allow one or two levels of internal appeal before you move to external review. Exactly how many levels you get depends on your specific plan and state, so check your denial letter or plan documents for the exact appeal path.
Does appealing pause my bill or collections?
Not automatically. An active appeal does not guarantee your provider will pause billing or hold off on collections. Ask your provider's billing office directly whether they'll place the balance on hold while your appeal is pending, and get that agreement in writing if possible.
