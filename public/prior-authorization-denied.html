Prior Authorization Denied?
Do These Two Things First

Prior Authorization

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

Your doctor ordered a treatment, procedure, or medication. Before you could get it, your insurance required advance approval — prior authorization. And they said no.

Most guides tell you to "appeal the denial" and stop there. But two things matter more than the appeal itself, and both happen before you file anything: requesting peer-to-peer review in the right order, and checking whether the denial happened because your doctor's office — not your medical condition — missed a deadline. Get those two things right and you can often resolve a denial in days instead of weeks. See our full guide on how to appeal an insurance denial for the formal appeal mechanics once you've done both.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Can I request peer-to-peer review and file a formal appeal at the same time?
You can, but you shouldn't need to. Peer-to-peer review is meant to happen first, precisely because it doesn't use up your formal appeal window. Filing both simultaneously usually just means you've skipped the faster option.
What is a peer-to-peer review?
A direct call between your doctor and the insurer's medical reviewer. Your doctor makes the clinical case in real time, which resolves many denials faster than a written appeal ever could.
Why would my prior authorization get denied even though my treatment is medically necessary?
Because prior auth denials aren't always about your medical need. A large share come from process failures — a missed submission deadline or a coding error — that have nothing to do with whether your doctor was right.
Why prior auth gets denied

The most common reasons

Understanding the specific reason your request was denied determines which fix actually works — a clinical denial and a process denial need completely different responses. Check your explanation of benefits or denial letter for the exact code.

  • Your doctor's office missed the response window — Insurers often ask for additional records within a short window after the initial request. If that deadline passes with no response, the request is denied automatically — regardless of medical need. Most explainers bury this inside generic "missing documentation" language instead of naming it as its own, separately preventable cause.
  • Not medically necessary — the insurer's clinical guidelines don't support the treatment for your diagnosis, at least as submitted. Read more on what "not medically necessary" actually means.
  • Step therapy required — your plan requires trying a cheaper treatment first, also called "fail first."
  • Wrong diagnosis or procedure code — a billing error can trigger an automatic denial even when the treatment itself is appropriate.
  • Not a covered benefit — the treatment is excluded from your plan entirely, regardless of medical need.
  • Out-of-network provider — the provider isn't in your network and your plan requires in-network care. See in-network vs. out-of-network coverage for how this affects approval.
  • Experimental or investigational classification — your insurer hasn't classified the treatment as proven, even if your doctor considers it standard of care.
Two clocks, not one

Peer-to-peer window vs. formal appeal window

This is where most people lose time they didn't need to lose. Peer-to-peer review and your formal appeal run on two separate clocks — confusing them is the single most common reason people skip the faster option.

Peer-to-peer window

Typically 7–14 days from the denial, though this varies by insurer and plan. Your denial letter should state the exact deadline — if it doesn't, call and ask the same day.

Formal appeal window

Usually up to 180 days from the denial. Requesting peer-to-peer review first does not shorten this window — the two clocks run independently.

⚠️ Don't wait to find out which clock applies

Because the peer-to-peer window is so much shorter, treating it like it's part of your 180-day appeal window is the mistake that costs people their best option. Confirm both deadlines from your insurer directly, in writing, before deciding what to do next.

The right order to do this in

Step by step

  1. Get the denial in writing. The letter must state the specific reason and the clinical criteria used. If you only got a phone call, request the written denial immediately.
  2. Call your insurer and ask two things: the peer-to-peer deadline, and the dates on file for your original request. The second question tells you whether your doctor's office missed a response window — ask specifically for the date any additional information was requested and the date it was due.
  3. Compare those dates against your doctor's office records the same day. If the office missed the window, this is usually a fast fix — a resubmission with the missing records, not a full appeal.
  4. Request peer-to-peer review before doing anything else, if your denial reason is medical necessity or clinical criteria. This step does not use any of your formal appeal time, so there's no downside to doing it first.
  5. If peer-to-peer isn't offered, or it fails, file your formal internal appeal. Include your doctor's letter of medical necessity, clinical notes, and supporting documentation. See our full appeal walkthrough for exactly what to include.
  6. If the internal appeal also fails, request an external review. An independent organization reviews the case, and their decision is binding on your insurer under federal law.
  7. File a complaint with your state insurance commissioner in parallel. This often accelerates resolution and creates a formal record, especially useful if you later need to show a documented pattern of denials.
What to do right now
Call your insurer today and ask two specific questions: how many days you have to request peer-to-peer review, and the exact dates on file for your original prior auth request. Loop in your doctor's office the same day with whatever you find. Doing this in the right order — peer-to-peer before the formal appeal, and checking for a missed response window before assuming it's a clinical denial — is what actually shortens how long this takes. Frequently asked questions
Should I request peer-to-peer review or file a formal appeal first?
Request peer-to-peer review first whenever your insurer offers it. It's faster, it puts your doctor directly in front of the insurer's medical reviewer, and requesting it does not use up any of your formal appeal window. Filing the formal appeal first, then trying peer-to-peer, is backwards and wastes your best early option.
How many days do I have to request a peer-to-peer review before I lose that option?
Most insurers give you 7 to 14 days from the denial to request peer-to-peer review, though the exact window depends on your plan and state. Your denial letter should state the deadline. If it doesn't, call your insurer the same day and ask directly — don't assume you have as long as your formal appeal window.
What if my doctor's office missed the insurer's deadline to respond to the prior authorization request?
This is a specific, preventable cause of denial that's different from a clinical judgment call. Insurers often set a short window for your doctor's office to submit additional records after an initial request, and if that window is missed, the request gets denied automatically — regardless of medical necessity. Most guides fold this into generic "missing documentation" language instead of naming it directly.
How can I tell if my doctor's office missed that window?
Call your insurer and ask for the exact date the prior authorization request was received, whether additional information was requested, and the date it was due. Then compare that against your doctor's office records. If the dates don't line up, that's your answer — and it's usually a fast fix once flagged.
If my peer-to-peer review is denied, do I still have full time left to file the formal appeal?
Yes. Peer-to-peer review runs on a separate, shorter clock from your formal appeal window, so a denied peer-to-peer review does not shorten your remaining time to file. Confirm the exact formal appeal deadline stated in your written denial letter, since it varies by insurer and plan.
