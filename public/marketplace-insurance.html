Marketplace Insurance
Explained

Health Insurance

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated August 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

Marketplace insurance is health coverage you buy on your own, outside of a job, through the exchanges the Affordable Care Act created. You shop plans, see what you'd pay after any tax credit, and enroll through HealthCare.gov or your state's own marketplace, depending on where you live.

2026 is a rougher year to be shopping than it's been in a while. The extra premium tax credits that made coverage cheaper for millions of households since 2021 expired at the end of 2025, so a lot of people are seeing higher monthly costs even if their income and plan haven't changed. On top of that, the rule that used to cap how much you had to repay if your subsidy estimate turned out too generous has also gone away, which means getting your income estimate right matters more this year than it used to.

None of this means marketplace coverage is a bad option — for many people without access to a job-based plan, it's still the most realistic way to get covered. It just means the numbers you remember from a prior year may not hold up, and it's worth re-checking before you assume anything.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is marketplace insurance the same as Obamacare?
Yes, they're the same thing. "Marketplace insurance," "ACA plans," and "Obamacare" all refer to coverage sold through the exchanges created by the Affordable Care Act. The name changes depending on who's talking, the product doesn't.
Can I get marketplace insurance with a pre-existing condition?
Yes. Marketplace plans can't deny you coverage or charge you more because of a health condition you already have. That protection is one of the core rules of the ACA and applies to every plan sold on the exchange.
What if I miss the enrollment window?
You may still have options. Certain life events open a Special Enrollment Period, and if none apply, short-term plans can bridge the gap — though they work differently and shouldn't be treated as a substitute for full coverage.
The basics

What a marketplace plan actually covers

Every marketplace plan, regardless of insurer or state, has to cover the same set of essential health benefits — things like doctor visits, hospital care, prescription drugs, mental health services, and maternity care. Where plans differ is cost-sharing: how much you pay out of pocket versus how much the plan covers, which shows up as your deductible, copays, and coinsurance.

  • Metal tiers — plans are grouped into Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum. Bronze has the lowest monthly premium but the highest costs when you actually use care; Platinum flips that.
  • Silver plans and subsidies — cost-sharing reductions, a second type of savings on top of the premium tax credit, are only available on Silver plans.
  • Networks — most marketplace plans use a limited network of doctors and hospitals, so checking who's in-network matters more here than with many employer plans.
  • Prescription coverage — every plan has a drug formulary, and it's worth checking your specific medications before assuming they're covered at a given tier.
  • Preventive care — annual checkups, screenings, and many vaccines are covered at no cost on every marketplace plan, before you hit your deductible.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum — every plan caps what you'll pay in a year, after which the plan covers 100% of covered care.
Comparison

Marketplace vs. employer coverage

If you have the option of both, it's worth understanding what actually changes between the two, beyond just the name on the card.

Employer plan

Your employer pays part of the premium and usually picks the plan options for you. Coverage is group-rated, so your individual health history doesn't affect your price.

Marketplace plan

You pay the full premium yourself, offset by a tax credit if you qualify. You choose from every plan available in your area instead of a short employer-selected list.

⚠️ THE "AFFORDABLE COVERAGE" RULE

If your employer offers a plan the IRS considers affordable and adequate, you generally can't also claim marketplace subsidies, even if you'd rather shop the exchange. Check your offer letter before assuming you have a real choice between the two. If you're weighing whether to enroll at all right now, it also helps to know what happens if you miss the enrollment window entirely.

Getting started

How to enroll in a marketplace plan

  1. Check your eligibility. Confirm you're not eligible for Medicare, Medicaid, or an affordable employer plan before shopping the exchange.
  2. Gather your documents. You'll need income estimates, household size, and Social Security numbers for everyone applying.
  3. Estimate your subsidy. Use the marketplace's built-in calculator to see your tax credit before you start comparing plans — it changes which tier makes sense.
  4. Check provider networks. Before picking a plan on price alone, confirm your doctors are actually in-network, since going out-of-network can erase any savings from a cheaper premium.
  5. Compare at least three plans. Look past the premium to the deductible, out-of-pocket max, and drug formulary before deciding.
  6. Enroll and pay your first premium. Coverage doesn't start until your first payment goes through, even after you've selected a plan.
What to do right now
If you're already enrolled, log back into your account and re-check your subsidy estimate — the rules changed enough between 2025 and 2026 that last year's numbers may no longer be accurate. If you're shopping for the first time, start with the subsidy calculator before you look at plans, so you're comparing real out-of-pocket costs instead of sticker prices. And if you're outside the enrollment window with no qualifying life event, look into whether a short-term plan makes sense as a temporary bridge rather than going uninsured.
Frequently asked questions
What is marketplace insurance?
Marketplace insurance is health coverage you buy directly, outside of an employer, through the exchanges created by the Affordable Care Act. You compare plans, see your subsidy amount, and enroll through HealthCare.gov or your state's own marketplace.
How much does marketplace insurance cost in 2026?
Costs vary by income, household size, and state, but 2026 is a notably more expensive year for many households. The extra subsidies that lowered premiums since 2021 expired at the end of 2025, and the cap that limited how much you had to repay if your subsidy estimate was too generous has also been removed. Check your exact numbers before assuming last year's premium still applies.
Who qualifies for marketplace insurance?
Most U.S. citizens and lawfully present residents who aren't eligible for Medicare, Medicaid, or affordable employer coverage can enroll. Subsidy eligibility depends on your household income relative to the federal poverty level.
What's the difference between marketplace insurance and employer insurance?
Employer coverage is typically group-rated and partly paid for by your employer, while marketplace plans are individually rated and any subsidy comes from federal tax credits instead. If you're offered "affordable" employer coverage as defined by the IRS, you generally can't also claim marketplace subsidies.
When is Open Enrollment for marketplace insurance?
Open Enrollment for marketplace plans generally runs in the fall for coverage starting the following January. Outside that window, you can only enroll if you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period after a life event like losing other coverage, moving, or having a baby.
