"Rated" vs "Denied"
What Medigap Underwriting Really Means

Medicare

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

Most people treat Medigap underwriting like a pass/fail test — you either get approved or you don't. That's not how it actually works. Underwriting produces two very different outcomes, and confusing them is what catches applicants off guard.

Being rated means the insurer approves you, just at a higher premium than someone in perfect health. Being denied means the insurer rejects the application outright, usually because a specific condition triggered what's called a health knockout. Someone who expects a modest surcharge and instead gets a flat denial — or vice versa — is reacting to a gap in expectations, not a gap in the rules.

Which outcome you get depends heavily on which specific carrier you apply to, not just your underlying health. Two applicants with identical pre-existing conditions can get completely different results from two different insurers, because each company sets its own underwriting guide.

Quick answers

What people ask most

What does it mean to be "rated" for Medigap?
You're approved, but at a higher premium. The insurer places you in a substandard rating class based on your health history instead of the standard or preferred tier.
What does a Medigap "denial" mean?
The insurer refuses to issue you a policy at all. This usually happens because a condition on your application matches an item on that carrier's knockout list.
If one carrier denies me, will they all?
Not necessarily. Knockout conditions vary by insurer, so a denial from one company doesn't predict the outcome with a different one.
Can I know in advance whether I'll be rated or denied?
Often, yes. Most carriers publish their underwriting guides, which list the specific conditions that trigger a rating versus an outright decline.
The real distinction

Rated vs. denied, condition by condition

Underwriting outcomes generally fall into a handful of tiers, and understanding where a condition typically lands changes how you approach the application.

  • Preferred or standard rating — you're approved at the carrier's best available price, usually reserved for applicants with no significant health flags.
  • Substandard rating ("rated") — you're approved, but priced higher, often for manageable chronic conditions like controlled diabetes or treated high blood pressure.
  • Health knockout ("denied") — the application is rejected outright, typically for conditions like a recent heart attack, active cancer treatment, or dialysis.
  • Carrier-specific thresholds — one insurer's knockout list might flag a condition that a competitor rates instead of declining. Comparing plan letters like Plan G vs. Plan N matters less here than comparing underwriting guides.
  • Stacked conditions — a single manageable condition might only trigger a rating, but two or three combined can push an application into denial territory even if none would individually.
  • Recency matters — a hospitalization or diagnosis within the past 6–24 months carries more weight than the same condition managed for years without incident.

This is also why timing your application around a move or a job change matters. Someone who recently switched from Medicare Advantage back to Medigap outside a protected window is a common example of an applicant who ends up facing full underwriting without realizing it in advance.

Side by side

Rated vs. denied — and how to skip underwriting entirely

Here's what separates the two outcomes, plus the protections that let you avoid underwriting in the first place.

Rated

Approved. Higher premium based on health history. You keep coverage, just at a worse price than standard.

Denied

Rejected outright. No coverage from that carrier. You'll need to apply elsewhere or wait for a protected window.

⚠️ THE ESCAPE HATCHES

Three protections let you skip underwriting entirely: the one-time 6-month federal Open Enrollment Period at 65, 63-day guaranteed issue windows tied to specific qualifying events like losing employer coverage, and state-level birthday rules. As of 2026, roughly 16 states offer a birthday rule — California gives you 60 days after your birthday, Oregon gives 30 days before and after, and Idaho gives 63 days from your birthday. A few states, including New York and Connecticut, go further with year-round guaranteed issue.

Before you apply

How to tell in advance whether you'll be rated or denied

  1. Check your timing first. If you're still inside your federal Open Enrollment window or a guaranteed issue period, underwriting doesn't apply at all — skip straight to applying. If you already missed open enrollment, underwriting is likely in play.
  2. Pull the carrier's underwriting guide. Most insurers publish a PDF listing exactly which conditions trigger a rating versus a decline. Request it before you apply, not after.
  3. List every condition, medication, and hospitalization from the past two years. This is what the questionnaire will ask for, so having it ready avoids omissions that can complicate a claim later.
  4. Cross-check your list against the knockout conditions. Recent cardiac events, active cancer treatment, and dialysis appear on nearly every carrier's decline list. Controlled chronic conditions usually land in "rated" territory instead.
  5. Check your state's protections. A birthday rule or year-round guaranteed issue rule can make the entire exercise unnecessary if you already hold a Medigap policy in that state.
  6. Don't confuse this with your Part B enrollment timing. Underwriting for Medigap is separate from the Part B late enrollment penalty — missing one doesn't trigger the other, but both are timing-sensitive in their own way.
  7. Apply strategically if you're outside protection. A denial from one carrier doesn't disqualify you everywhere — different companies weigh the same conditions differently.
What to do right now
Confirm whether you're still inside a protected enrollment window before assuming underwriting applies to you at all. If you're comparing Medigap against staying on a spouse's plan, see how it stacks up against employer coverage first. If underwriting does apply, request underwriting guides from two or three carriers before submitting anything — it's the fastest way to know where you'll land before you have a decision on record.
Frequently asked questions
What's the difference between being "rated" and "denied" for a Medigap policy?
Being rated means the insurer approves your application but charges a higher premium because of your health history. Being denied means the insurer rejects your application outright, usually because of a specific condition on their knockout list. Rated still gets you covered; denied means you have to try elsewhere.
Can I avoid Medigap underwriting entirely?
Yes. Applying during your one-time 6-month federal Medigap Open Enrollment Period, during a 63-day guaranteed issue window tied to a qualifying event, or during a state-level protection like a birthday rule or year-round guaranteed issue lets you skip underwriting completely.
What health conditions most commonly get a Medigap application denied?
Conditions frequently flagged include recent heart attack or bypass surgery, active cancer treatment, kidney failure or dialysis, and recent hospitalizations. Exact knockout conditions vary by carrier, so a condition that denies you with one insurer may not with another.
What is the Medigap birthday rule, and which states have it?
The birthday rule is a state-level law giving existing Medigap policyholders an annual window around their birthday to switch plans without underwriting. As of 2026, roughly 16 states offer some version of it, including California, Oregon, and Idaho, each with different window lengths.
What happens if I'm denied by one Medigap carrier — can I try another?
Yes. Underwriting standards are set by each individual carrier, not shared across the industry. A denial from one insurer does not automatically disqualify you with another, so applying to a second carrier with different knockout criteria is a normal next step.
