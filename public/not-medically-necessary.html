Insurer Said "Not Medically Necessary"?
Here's What That Actually Means

Claims & Denials

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

"Not medically necessary" is one of the most common reasons insurers give for denying a claim — and one of the most misleading, because it gets used as a catch-all label even when the real issue is something else entirely. Your doctor recommended the treatment. Your insurer's internal reviewer, often without ever speaking to your doctor, decided it didn't meet the plan's specific criteria for coverage.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. A true medical-necessity denial is one of the strongest categories to appeal — internal appeal success rates run in the 30–40% range, and independent external review overturns roughly half of the cases that reach it. But a denial mislabeled as "medical necessity" when it's actually a coverage exclusion follows a completely different, much narrower path. Getting that distinction wrong is the single biggest reason people give up on appeals they could have won.

This guide breaks down how to tell which kind of denial you actually have, and lays out the exact deadline chain — from your first appeal to a binding independent decision — that most explanations leave scattered across three or four separate government pages.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Can my doctor's recommendation override a "not medically necessary" denial?
Not automatically, but it's your strongest evidence. Insurers apply their own clinical criteria, which can be stricter than general medical practice. A detailed letter from your doctor addressing the specific denial reason — not just repeating that treatment is needed — is the core of almost every successful appeal.
How often do medical necessity denials actually get overturned?
More often than most people expect. Federal marketplace data shows internal appeals overturn a meaningful share of denials, and independent external reviews overturn roughly half of the cases that reach that stage. The catch: fewer than 1 in 500 denied claims are ever appealed at all.
Do I need a lawyer to appeal a medical necessity denial?
Usually no, for the first two stages. Internal appeals and standard external reviews are designed to be done by the patient with a supporting letter from their doctor. A lawyer becomes worth considering if the case involves large dollar amounts, an ERISA employer plan with rigid internal rules, or a denial you believe was made in bad faith.
The real distinction

Three different denials, one confusing label

Insurers sometimes stamp "not medically necessary" on denials that actually fall into a different category — and which category you're in changes your entire appeal strategy. Before you write a single word of your appeal, confirm which one applies by reading the exact policy section cited in your denial letter, not just the summary line. See how coverage exclusions work for the full breakdown of the exclusion path.

  • True medical necessity denial — the service is a covered benefit, but the insurer's reviewer decided your specific case didn't meet their clinical criteria. Fully appealable through internal appeal and external review.
  • Coverage exclusion — the service is categorically excluded from your policy regardless of medical need (cosmetic procedures, certain weight-loss treatments, some fertility services). Not eligible for the medical-necessity external review path — your argument has to be that the exclusion doesn't apply to your situation.
  • Experimental or investigational — a separate label from medical necessity, but treated similarly for appeal rights. You'll need clinical trial data or peer-reviewed evidence to support the appeal, not just your doctor's opinion.
  • Insufficient documentation — the most common and easiest denial to fix. Your provider's notes didn't include enough clinical detail to meet the criteria, even though the treatment itself would qualify.
  • Frequency or duration limit exceeded — common with physical therapy, imaging, or mental health visits. The insurer's cap was hit, not a judgment about whether you need more care.
  • Algorithm-flagged, never reviewed by a person — some denials happen automatically based on coding, with no human review of your actual case. A peer-to-peer conversation between your doctor and the plan's medical director often resolves this fastest.
Deadline chain

The four stages, and the exact clock on each one

This is the part almost every source splits across separate pages. Here's the full chain in one place, for ACA-regulated plans (Medicare Advantage and some employer plans differ — see the FAQ below).

Stage 1 — Internal appeal

File within 180 days of your denial notice. Insurer must respond within 30 days (pre-service) or 60 days (post-service), or 72 hours if you qualify for expedited review.

Stage 2 — External review

If the internal appeal is denied, file for external review within 4 months of that final denial (some states, like California, allow up to 6 months).

⚠️ THE DEADLINE THAT GETS MISSED

The 4-month external review window starts from your final internal denial, not your original denial. People who don't realize a second, formal "final" letter is coming often let this clock run out before they even know it started.

Stage 3 — External review decision: a standard review is decided within 45 days; an expedited review within 72 hours. It's conducted by an Independent Review Organization with no financial relationship to your insurer, and its decision is legally binding on the plan.

Stage 4 — Cost: the federal external review process is free. State-run programs can charge a fee, but it's capped at $25 and is typically refunded if you win.

Action plan

What to do in order

  1. Read the exact denial reason, including the specific policy section or clinical criteria cited — not just the one-line summary. This determines which of the six denial types above you're actually dealing with.
  2. Request your complete claim file from the insurer, including the specific clinical guideline used to deny you. You're entitled to this, and it tells you exactly what your appeal needs to overcome.
  3. Get a detailed letter from your doctor that directly rebuts the denial reason, cites treatment guidelines where possible, and documents any alternatives you've already tried and failed. Learn more about what a strong appeal letter includes.
  4. Request a peer-to-peer review alongside your formal appeal if your denial letter doesn't already show one happened — this step alone resolves a meaningful share of automated or under-reviewed denials fast.
  5. File your internal appeal well before the 180-day deadline, and mark your calendar the day the denial letter arrives, not when you get around to reading it.
  6. If denied again, file for external review within 4 months of that final letter. Check your Explanation of Benefits and denial letter for the specific reviewer contact information — it varies by state.
What to do right now
Find the exact clinical reason on your denial letter today, not the summary phrase. If it references a policy exclusion rather than a clinical criterion, you're on the exclusion track, not the medical-necessity track — check here before you build your appeal around the wrong argument.
Frequently asked questions
Does a denial letter always correctly state the real reason?
Not always. Insurers sometimes label a denial "not medically necessary" when the real issue is a coverage exclusion or missing documentation. Read the specific policy language cited in your denial letter, not just the headline reason, before you decide how to appeal.
Can I get an expedited appeal if my situation is urgent but not a life-threatening emergency?
Yes, if waiting for a standard decision would seriously jeopardize your health, function, or ability to regain maximum function. Your insurer makes the initial urgency call, but your doctor can submit a statement supporting expedited status, and you can push back if the insurer wrongly denies it.
If I win an external review, does that guarantee future claims for the same treatment get approved?
No. An external review decision is binding for the specific claim or episode under review, not a blanket approval for ongoing treatment. If you need the same service again later, especially after a plan year change, you may need to submit a new prior authorization or claim.
What happens if my insurer misses its own appeal deadline?
Under ACA rules, if a plan fails to strictly comply with internal appeal deadlines and procedures, your internal appeal is generally considered exhausted automatically. That means you may be able to skip straight to external review even though the insurer never issued a final decision.
Is the appeal process different for Medicare Advantage plans?
Yes. Medicare Advantage denials don't go to an Independent Review Organization. Instead, an unfavorable internal appeal escalates to a Qualified Independent Contractor (QIC) review, and from there to an Administrative Law Judge hearing if the amount in dispute meets the federal threshold.
