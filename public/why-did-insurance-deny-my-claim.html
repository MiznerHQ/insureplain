Why Did My Insurance
Deny My Claim?

Claims & Denials

What's actually happening

A denied claim is not the same thing as a slow claim or a partially paid one. A denial is a formal decision by your insurer that it will not pay for a specific service, and it comes with a written reason and — in almost every case — a right to challenge it. That distinction matters, because the right next step depends entirely on which situation you're actually in.

A deductible that hasn't been met yet, or a claim still sitting in processing, can look identical to a denial on your insurance portal at first glance. The difference is that a delay eventually resolves on its own, while a denial requires you to act — usually within a fixed window — or the decision becomes final.

Every denial letter is required to include a specific reason code and instructions for appeal. That letter is the single most important document you'll receive in this process, because everything about how you respond depends on exactly why the claim was denied.

It also helps to know what a denial is not. It isn't the same as hitting your out-of-pocket maximum, which caps what you owe rather than blocking payment entirely. And it isn't a billing dispute over your copay or coinsurance share — those calculations only kick in once a claim has already been approved. A denial specifically means the insurer decided, at least for now, that it won't pay any portion of that particular claim.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is a claim denial different from a claim delay?
Yes — a delay is temporary, a denial is a formal decision. A delay means the insurer is still reviewing or waiting on information. A denial means a decision has already been made not to pay, and it comes with an appeal deadline.
Can I still get paid after a denial?
Often, yes — through an appeal or corrected resubmission. Many denials are overturned once missing information is provided or an error is corrected, so a denial is rarely the final word.
How long do I have to respond to a denial?
The deadline is printed on your denial letter and varies by state and plan. 180 days is common for many health plans, but shorter windows apply to some policy types, so check your specific letter first.
Common causes

Why claims actually get denied

Most denials fall into a small number of recurring categories. Knowing which one applies to you determines whether you're fixing a paperwork problem or fighting a coverage decision. Related costs like your copay or coinsurance don't factor into whether a claim is approved — those only apply after a claim is already accepted.

  • Missing prior authorization — the service required pre-approval that wasn't obtained before treatment began.
  • Out-of-network provider — the provider or facility wasn't part of your plan's network, so no negotiated rate applied.
  • Coding or billing errors — the provider's office submitted an incorrect procedure or diagnosis code, which is easier to fix than it sounds.
  • Not medically necessary — the insurer's reviewer determined the service didn't meet its own medical necessity criteria for that diagnosis.
  • Policy exclusion — the specific service or condition is excluded under your plan's terms entirely, regardless of medical necessity.
  • Coverage lapse or eligibility issue — a gap tied to something like a missed enrollment window or a lapsed premium payment that ended active coverage.

Some of these are administrative and get fixed in days. Others are genuine coverage decisions that require a real case to overturn, which is exactly why identifying the category first matters more than reacting immediately.

Two different problems

Correctable error vs. coverage decision

Every denial is either a mistake that can be fixed or a genuine coverage decision the insurer stands behind. Telling them apart before you respond saves time and improves your odds. This is separate from questions about your deductible — a claim can be denied whether or not your deductible has already been met for the year.

Correctable error

Wrong billing code, missing documentation, or a clerical mismatch. Usually fixed with a corrected resubmission, not a formal appeal.

Coverage decision

The insurer reviewed the claim and determined it isn't covered under your policy's terms. This requires a formal appeal, not a resubmission.

⚠️ Appeal deadlines are strict

Once the appeal window on your denial letter closes, the decision typically becomes final and cannot be reopened, even if you had a valid case. Note the deadline the day you receive the letter.

Your next move

How to respond to a denial

  1. Read the denial letter fully. Find the specific reason code and the appeal deadline before doing anything else.
  2. Call your insurer to confirm the reason. Ask them to explain the denial in plain language and confirm what documentation would change it.
  3. Gather supporting records. Medical notes, itemized bills, and prior authorization confirmations all strengthen an appeal.
  4. Choose resubmission or appeal. A billing error goes back to your provider for correction; a coverage decision goes to a formal written appeal.
  5. Submit in writing and keep copies. Phone calls alone rarely count as an official appeal — follow up with a written request.
  6. Track the response deadline. Insurers are required to respond to appeals within a set timeframe, which your denial letter or state insurance department can confirm.
What to do right now
Pull up your denial letter and find the reason code and appeal deadline first — everything else follows from those two details. If the deadline is close, file a written appeal now and gather supporting documents afterward rather than waiting until your case is fully built.
Frequently asked questions
Can a claim be denied for a pre-existing condition?
Under the ACA, most health plans can't deny claims for pre-existing conditions, but this rule doesn't apply to every policy type. Short-term health plans, some disability policies, and certain supplemental products can still exclude pre-existing conditions, so the answer depends on what kind of policy you hold.
Why was my claim denied for going out-of-network?
Most plans pay a lower rate — or nothing at all — for providers outside their network, and a full denial usually means the plan has no out-of-network benefit at all. Emergency care is a major exception, since federal law generally requires it to be covered at in-network rates regardless of where you're treated.
What happens if I skip prior authorization?
If your plan requires prior authorization for a service and it wasn't obtained before treatment, the insurer can deny the claim even if the treatment itself was medically necessary. Some plans allow a retroactive authorization request, but it isn't guaranteed, which is why checking requirements beforehand matters.
How long do I have to appeal a denied claim?
Appeal deadlines vary by state and by policy type, but 180 days from the denial notice is a common window for employer and marketplace health plans. Some states set shorter windows for specific policy types, so the exact deadline is always printed on your denial letter.
Can I resubmit a claim after it's denied instead of appealing?
Yes — if the denial was caused by a simple error like a wrong billing code or missing paperwork, a corrected resubmission is often faster than a formal appeal. If the denial was based on a coverage decision rather than an error, an appeal is usually the correct path instead.
