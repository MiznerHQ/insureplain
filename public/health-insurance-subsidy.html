What Is a Health Insurance Subsidy
And What You Could Owe Back

Health Insurance

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional or tax preparer about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

A health insurance subsidy is government help that lowers what you pay for a Marketplace health plan — often the first thing people look into right after they've lost employer coverage. Most explanations stop there — but a subsidy isn't a gift card. It's an advance on a tax credit, and it gets settled up when you file your taxes.

If the income you estimated when you enrolled turns out to be lower than your actual income for the year, you were paid too much subsidy — and starting with 2026 tax returns, you have to pay every dollar of that excess back, with no cap on the amount.

The other piece almost nobody puts a number on: the income cliff. The temporary rules that let higher earners qualify expired on January 1, 2026, so the old hard cutoff is back — cross it by even a dollar and your entire subsidy disappears.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Do I have to pay back a health insurance subsidy?
You might, and there's no longer a limit on how much. If your actual income for the year ends up higher than you estimated, you'll owe back the difference between what you received in advance and what you actually qualified for.
What income makes you eligible for a subsidy in 2026?
Generally 100% to 400% of the federal poverty level. For 2026 coverage in the 48 contiguous states, that upper limit works out to roughly $62,600 for a single person and $128,600 for a family of four.
Is a subsidy the same as a tax credit?
Yes — "subsidy" and "premium tax credit" describe the same benefit. When it's paid monthly toward your premium in advance, it's called the advance premium tax credit (APTC); when claimed at tax time, it's the premium tax credit (PTC).
The basics

The two types of subsidy

There are two separate forms of help available through the health insurance Marketplace, and they work differently.

  • Premium tax credit (PTC) — lowers your monthly premium. Most people take it in advance (APTC) so it's applied directly to each month's bill instead of waiting to claim it on a tax return.
  • Cost-sharing reduction (CSR) — lowers your deductible, copays, and out-of-pocket maximum. Only available if you pick a Silver plan and your income is between 100% and 250% of the federal poverty level.
  • Eligibility window — generally 100% to 400% of the federal poverty level for your household size and state.
  • How the amount is set — based on your household income against the cost of the second-lowest-cost Silver plan (the "benchmark plan") where you live, not a flat dollar amount.
  • Who it applies to — only plans bought on the Marketplace. If you're comparing coverage after losing a job, this matters for anyone deciding between a Marketplace plan and COBRA continuation coverage, since COBRA never carries a subsidy.
  • What you estimate — your projected income for the coming year, not last year's tax return, which is exactly where the repayment risk starts.
The gap competitors skip

The repayment math, in real numbers

Through tax year 2025, if you underestimated your income, the amount you had to repay was capped based on your income tier — sometimes as low as a few hundred dollars. That protection is gone.

Through tax year 2025

Repayment capped by income tier — as little as roughly $375 up to about $3,250, depending on how your income compared to the poverty level.

Tax year 2026 onward

No cap at all. Every dollar of excess advance credit must be repaid in full, regardless of your income level, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

⚠️ THE 400% CLIFF IS BACK

From 2021 through 2025, there was no upper income limit on subsidies. That enhancement expired January 1, 2026. Now, one dollar over 400% of the federal poverty level and your credit drops to exactly zero — and any advance credit you already received for the year comes back in full. This is a cliff, not a slope: there's no partial phase-out.

Protect yourself

How to avoid a repayment surprise

  1. Estimate conservatively. If your income is uncertain — freelance work, overtime, a possible raise — lean toward a slightly higher estimate rather than a lower one.
  2. Update your Marketplace application the moment your income changes. Don't wait until tax season; a mid-year update adjusts your monthly credit going forward instead of building up an owed balance.
  3. Watch the 400% line closely if you're near it. A single bonus or a good freelance month can push you over and eliminate your entire subsidy for the year.
  4. Compare it against your other options. If you were recently dropped from employer coverage, run the numbers on what COBRA actually costs against a subsidized Marketplace plan before assuming the subsidized option is automatically cheaper.
  5. Factor plan network into the decision, not just price. A cheaper subsidized plan isn't a deal if your doctors are out-of-network and you're paying full price for care anyway.
  6. Set aside a buffer if you're close to any income threshold. Treat a potential repayment like a bill you might owe, not a hypothetical.
What to do right now
Log into your Marketplace account and check the income estimate currently on file. If your actual income for the year looks like it'll come in higher than that number, update it now rather than waiting for tax season — it's the single biggest lever you have over what you might owe. If you've recently had a premium change unrelated to income, our guide on handling a premium increase walks through what to check first.
Frequently asked questions
What happens if I underestimate my income and get too much subsidy?
You'll likely owe back some or all of the excess advance premium tax credit when you file your taxes. Starting with tax year 2026, there's no repayment cap on this amount, regardless of your income level, so an underestimate can mean a large, unexpected tax bill.
What happens if I overestimate my income? Do I get money back?
Yes. If your actual income comes in lower than what you estimated, you were likely eligible for a bigger credit than you received in advance. You'll get the difference back as a larger refund or a reduced tax bill when you reconcile on Form 8962.
Is there a cap on how much I have to repay?
Not anymore. Through tax year 2025, repayment was capped at a few hundred to a few thousand dollars for households under 400% of the federal poverty level. That cap was eliminated starting with tax year 2026, so any excess advance credit must be repaid in full.
What if my income lands right around the 400% FPL line?
That line is a hard cliff, not a slope. One dollar under 400% of the federal poverty level and you keep your credit; one dollar over and it drops to zero, and you'd owe back the full advance amount. If your income is close to this line, it's worth estimating conservatively and updating your Marketplace application the moment your income changes.
Do subsidies still work if I'm self-employed with variable income?
Yes, but the risk of a repayment surprise is higher because your income is harder to predict. The Marketplace uses your projected annual income, so a strong month or an unexpected contract can push your final number higher than what you estimated. Updating your income estimate mid-year whenever it shifts meaningfully is the main way to avoid a reconciliation surprise.
