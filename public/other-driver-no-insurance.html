The Other Driver Has No Insurance.
Here's What Actually Happens Next.

AUTO INSURANCE

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult your policy documents or a licensed insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

A police officer just told you the other driver has no insurance. Roughly 1 in 8 U.S. drivers is uninsured, and in some states that number climbs past 1 in 4 — so this situation is far more common than most people expect until it happens to them.

The problem is that most explainers stop at "you'll need uninsured motorist coverage" and move on. That leaves out the part that actually determines whether your claim gets paid: uninsured motorist (UM) coverage is real and it works, but it comes with a notification deadline that's often dramatically shorter than the lawsuit deadline everyone quotes — and if you don't have UM coverage at all, you have fewer options than the internet suggests, but you're not out of luck either.

This page walks through both clocks, what to do if you skipped UM coverage, and the exact steps to take in the first 30 days.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Will my insurance go up if the other driver was at fault?
Usually not, in most states. Insurers generally can't surcharge you for a not-at-fault claim, including one paid through your own UM coverage. Confirm this in writing with your specific carrier, since state rules and company policy both vary.
How long do I have to file a claim?
There are two separate deadlines, not one. Your policy's notification window (often 10–30 days) is much shorter than your state's statute of limitations for a lawsuit (typically 1–6 years). Missing the short one can void your claim before the long one is ever relevant.
What if I don't have uninsured motorist coverage?
You still have paths, just fewer of them. Health insurance, MedPay/PIP if you carry it, small claims court for minor property damage, and in some states a victim compensation fund can all apply. See the fallback breakdown below.
The two clocks nobody separates

Notification deadline vs. statute of limitations

Most articles on this topic quote your state's statute of limitations — the legal deadline to file a lawsuit, usually 1 to 6 years — and leave it there. That number is real, but it's not the deadline that actually kills most uninsured motorist claims.

  • Policy notification deadline — buried in your own policy's fine print, this is often 10–30 days from the accident, and in some states as short as 10 business days for hit-and-run claims. Miss it and your carrier can deny the UM claim outright, regardless of how much time is left on the statute of limitations.
  • State statute of limitations — the outer legal limit to formally file suit or demand arbitration, typically 1–3 years for injury claims and sometimes longer. This is the number every article leads with, which is exactly why people assume they have more time than they actually do.
  • Hit-and-run notice windows — frequently shorter and stricter than standard UM notice, sometimes requiring a police report and written notice within 10 business days to preserve your claim at all.
  • State-run victim funds — some states (New York's MVAIC is one example) run separate compensation programs for accidents involving uninsured or unidentified vehicles, with their own filing deadlines — 90 days for hit-and-run, 180 days for a confirmed uninsured vehicle, in New York's case.

Check your own declarations page or call your agent today and ask directly: "What is my policy's written notification deadline for an uninsured motorist claim?" Don't rely on the statute of limitations as your working deadline.

If you don't have UM coverage

Your fallback options, ranked by reliability

UM coverage is optional in roughly half of U.S. states. If you skipped it, or your policy limits are too low, here's what actually helps — and what usually doesn't.

Most reliable

Health insurance for medical bills, MedPay/PIP if you carry it, and collision coverage for your vehicle (subject to your own deductible) all pay out regardless of the other driver's insurance status.

Least reliable

Suing the uninsured driver directly is legally possible, but people without insurance frequently don't have meaningful assets to collect from — a court judgment doesn't guarantee actual payment.

⚠️ THE DEDUCTIBLE TRADE-OFF

Using your own collision coverage means paying your deductible upfront. If the other driver is later identified and found at fault, subrogation lets your insurer pursue reimbursement from them on your behalf — but that process can take months, and there's no guarantee of full recovery.

Action plan

What to do in the first 30 days

  1. Get a police report. This is your single most important piece of evidence, especially for hit-and-run or disputed-fault situations, and most insurers require it before paying a UM claim.
  2. Photograph everything. Vehicle damage, the scene, visible injuries, and the other driver's information if you can get it, even without valid insurance.
  3. Notify your own insurer within days, not weeks. Don't wait to "see how you feel" or gather every document first — call and state your intent to file a UM claim immediately, then follow up in writing.
  4. Ask your agent for your policy's written notification deadline, in writing. Don't rely on memory or a generic online article, including this one — your specific policy language controls.
  5. Check whether you have UMPD (property damage) separately from UMBI (bodily injury). Many policies only include one, and your declarations page will list them as separate line items.
  6. If you have no UM coverage, open a claim under health insurance, MedPay, or PIP immediately rather than waiting to see if the other driver "figures it out" — those coverages usually have their own short filing windows too.
What to do right now
Pull your policy's declarations page today and find your UM/UIM notification deadline in writing — don't rely on the statute of limitations as your working timeline. Frequently asked questions
Does it matter if the uninsured driver was driving someone else's car?
Usually not for your claim. Your uninsured motorist coverage responds to the fact that no valid liability insurance covers the at-fault driver, regardless of whose car they were driving. The car's owner may face separate liability questions with their own insurer, but that dispute doesn't change your path: you still file under your own UM coverage.
What if the uninsured driver fled the scene (hit-and-run)?
Hit-and-run claims are treated as uninsured motorist claims in most states, but the notification window is often shorter and stricter. Some states require a police report and formal notice to your insurer within as little as 10 business days. Report to police immediately and notify your insurer the same day if at all possible.
Can I sue the uninsured driver directly instead of using my insurance?
Yes, this is legally an option, but it rarely pays off. Someone without insurance frequently doesn't have meaningful assets either, and a court judgment doesn't guarantee actual payment. Small claims court can work for minor property damage within your state's dollar limit, but for injuries or major repair costs, your own uninsured motorist coverage is almost always the faster, more reliable path.
Does using uninsured motorist coverage raise my own rates?
In most states, insurers cannot surcharge you for using UM coverage after an accident that wasn't your fault, since you didn't cause the crash. Rules vary by state and insurer, so ask your agent directly and get the answer in writing before assuming either way.
What if I don't have collision coverage — does uninsured motorist coverage still pay for my car?
It depends on what you bought. Uninsured motorist bodily injury (UMBI) covers medical costs regardless of your collision coverage. Uninsured motorist property damage (UMPD) is a separate add-on in many states, and without it or collision coverage, vehicle repairs may not be covered at all. Check your declarations page for both UMBI and UMPD listed separately.
