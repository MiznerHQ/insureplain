What a Claims Adjuster
Actually Does

Claims & Denials

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

Once you file a claim, an adjuster gets assigned to investigate it, figure out what's covered, and decide how much the insurance company should pay. That much, every insurer's website will tell you plainly.

What most explanations leave out is the part that actually matters to you: a claims adjuster's job is to protect the insurance company's money, not yours. That's true even when the adjuster is polite, responsive, and seems to be on your side throughout the process. Their employer pays them to control claim costs, and that incentive shapes every estimate, every offer, and every question they ask you.

This doesn't mean adjusters are dishonest or that your claim is doomed. It means you're negotiating with someone whose job depends on paying out less than the maximum, so understanding how they work — and what leverage you actually have — is the difference between accepting the first number you're offered and getting what your policy actually owes you.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Does the claims adjuster work for me?
No, not unless you specifically hired a public adjuster. The adjuster assigned to your claim, whether a staff employee or an independent contractor, is working for and paid by the insurance company, and their goal is to settle the claim for as little as the policy and the facts allow.
Do I have to accept the first settlement offer?
No. A first offer is an opening position, not a final number. You can counter it with your own documentation, a second estimate, or by invoking your policy's appraisal clause if one exists.
Can an adjuster deny my claim on the spot?
Usually not immediately. Most denials go through a formal review process, and you typically have the right to appeal an insurance denial in writing with supporting evidence before it's final.
Who you might actually be dealing with

The three types of adjusters

The word "adjuster" covers three different roles, and knowing which one you're talking to changes how much weight to give what they tell you. Homeowners claims and auto claims commonly involve all three.

  • Staff adjuster — a salaried, full-time employee of your insurance company. They handle claims exclusively for that one insurer and follow its internal guidelines closely.
  • Independent adjuster — a contractor hired by an insurer, often brought in during high claim volume like after a hurricane or wildfire. They work for whichever company retains them on a given claim, but the incentive is the same: control the payout.
  • Public adjuster — the one type actually hired by you, the policyholder, to represent your interests and negotiate on your behalf. Public adjusters typically charge a percentage of your final settlement, often in the 5–15% range depending on the state and complexity of the claim.
  • Independent appraiser — sometimes brought in separately to estimate repair costs or property value; their estimate feeds into the adjuster's final number but they don't decide coverage.
  • Claims examiner — reviews the adjuster's work internally to confirm it follows the insurer's guidelines before a payout is finalized.
Whose interest they represent

Insurance-company adjuster vs. public adjuster

This is the comparison almost no explainer draws clearly, because most claims-adjuster content is written by insurance companies themselves.

Insurance company adjuster

Assigned automatically when you file. Paid by, and reports to, the insurer. Goal: settle for the lowest amount the policy and facts support. Free to you.

Public adjuster

Hired directly by you. Paid a percentage of your settlement. Goal: maximize your payout. Best for large, disputed, or complex losses.

⚠️ Watch the clock

Many states set a statute of limitations on disputing a settlement, and most policies require you to report new or additional damage within a set window after the original claim. If you're weighing a public adjuster or appraisal, don't wait — check how long your claim decision is legally allowed to take before you assume you have unlimited time to negotiate.

Protecting yourself

How to work with an adjuster without getting shortchanged

  1. Document everything before the adjuster arrives. Photos, videos, receipts, and a written timeline of what happened all strengthen your position and are harder to dispute than a verbal account.
  2. Know your policy limits and your deductible before you talk numbers. An adjuster who realizes you don't know your own coverage has more room to lowball you.
  3. Be careful with recorded statements, especially to someone else's insurer. You're not obligated to give a detailed recorded statement to the other party's adjuster in most situations.
  4. Get your own repair or replacement estimate. A second, independent number is your strongest leverage against a lowball offer.
  5. Put counteroffers in writing. Verbal negotiations are easy for an insurer to walk back; a written record isn't.
  6. Escalate if you're stuck. An unresolved disagreement over an estimate or a denial can move to a formal appeal, an appraisal clause, or a public adjuster or attorney for larger claims.
When a third party is involved

If more than one insurance company is in the picture

Some claims involve two adjusters from two different companies, especially after a car accident with another driver, or when your health insurer later seeks reimbursement from a liable third party's insurer. That reimbursement process is called subrogation, and it happens behind the scenes, often after your claim has already been paid.

If an adjuster who doesn't work for your own insurance company contacts you directly, treat the conversation carefully. Their job is to protect their employer's money, not yours, even if they're friendly and the conversation feels routine.

What to do right now
Before your next call with an adjuster, write down your policy limits, your deductible, and a rough estimate of your loss based on your own research or a second quote. Going in prepared is the single biggest thing that changes how a negotiation goes.
Frequently asked questions
Can I refuse to give a recorded statement to a claims adjuster?
Yes, in most cases. You're generally required to cooperate with your own insurer's investigation under your policy's terms, but that doesn't always mean a recorded statement is mandatory, and you're not required to give one to the other driver's or the other party's insurance company at all. If you're unsure, ask the adjuster to point to the exact policy language requiring it, and consider having an attorney present for anything beyond basic facts.
What if an adjuster for the other party's insurance contacts me?
Be cautious. That adjuster works for the other party's insurer, not for you, and their job is to minimize what their company pays out, sometimes by getting you to say something that can be used to reduce or deny your claim. You're not obligated to give them a recorded statement, and it's reasonable to direct them to your own insurance company or attorney instead.
Can I negotiate directly with a claims adjuster, or do I need a lawyer?
You can negotiate directly for straightforward, lower-dollar claims, especially if you've documented the damage well and understand your policy limits. For large, disputed, or complicated losses, such as a denied claim, a serious injury, or a lowball settlement offer, a public adjuster or attorney can often recover more than their fee costs, since they know the negotiation tactics adjusters use.
Does it matter if I'm dealing with a staff adjuster or an independent adjuster?
It changes who they answer to, but not their basic incentive. A staff adjuster is a salaried employee of your insurance company, while an independent adjuster is a contractor hired by the insurer, often during high claim volume like after a natural disaster. Both are working on behalf of the insurance company that hired them, not on behalf of you as the policyholder.
What can I do if I disagree with the adjuster's damage estimate?
Get your own written estimate from a contractor, repair shop, or independent appraiser and submit it in writing along with photos and documentation supporting the higher figure. Many policies also include an appraisal clause that lets you demand a neutral, binding appraisal process if you and the insurer can't agree, and you can escalate to a formal appeal if the estimate is tied to a denial.
