In-Network vs. Out-of-Network
What It Actually Costs You

Health Insurance

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

In-network means a provider signed a contract with your insurer agreeing to accept a set, discounted rate for their services. Out-of-network means no such contract exists, so the provider can charge whatever they want.

That part is easy. What almost nobody explains clearly is what happens to the leftover money when a provider's charge is higher than what your insurer decides is reasonable. That gap has a name — the allowed amount — and whether you get stuck paying it depends on your plan type, the situation, and a federal law that changed the rules in 2022.

This guide walks through the actual math, when you're protected from the difference, and how to check network status before a bill shows up instead of after.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Does out-of-network mean I'm not covered at all?
Not necessarily. Many plans still pay something toward out-of-network care, just at a lower percentage and often only after a separate, higher deductible. Some plan types, like HMOs and EPOs, pay nothing for non-emergency out-of-network care.
Can my in-network doctor bill me an out-of-network rate?
No, not for their own services. An in-network provider agreed to the negotiated rate for everything they personally bill. Trouble usually comes from other providers involved in your care, like a lab or anesthesiologist, who may not be in-network even at an in-network facility.
Is a hospital in-network if my doctor is in-network?
Not automatically. Facilities and the individual providers working inside them are contracted separately. An in-network surgeon can operate at an out-of-network hospital, and an in-network hospital can have out-of-network anesthesiologists on staff.
Will my explanation of benefits show if I was balance billed?
Yes, if you read it closely. Look for a line separating the "allowed amount" from the provider's billed charge — that difference is what an out-of-network provider may try to collect from you directly.
The math behind the bill

How the allowed amount actually works

Say an MRI is billed at $2,500. Your insurer decides the reasonable, negotiated cost for that scan is $900 — that $900 is the allowed amount. Here's where in-network and out-of-network split:

  • In-network: the provider agreed in advance to accept $900 as payment in full. You owe only your coinsurance or copay share of that $900. The remaining $1,600 gets written off — the provider can't come after you for it.
  • Out-of-network, unprotected: the provider never agreed to $900. Your insurer might still pay a portion of it, say $400, but the provider can legally bill you for the rest — in this example, up to $2,100. That's balance billing.
  • Out-of-network, protected by law: in specific situations covered by the federal No Surprises Act, the provider is barred from balance billing you even though they're out-of-network. You pay only your normal in-network cost-share.
  • Whether you land in the second or third bucket depends entirely on the circumstances of the visit, not on how the bill looks.

If a bill like this lands in your mailbox and you're not sure which bucket you're in, our guide to handling a surprise medical bill walks through what to check first.

When you're protected

The No Surprises Act, in plain English

Since January 2022, federal law blocks balance billing in three specific situations, no matter what your plan normally covers out-of-network:

Protected

Emergency room visits at any hospital. Air ambulance transport. Care from an out-of-network provider (anesthesiologist, radiologist, assistant surgeon) at an in-network facility, when you didn't choose them.

Not protected

Choosing an out-of-network doctor or specialist on purpose. Non-emergency care at an out-of-network facility. Ground ambulance rides, which the law doesn't cover.

⚠️ It's not automatic paperwork

Providers and insurers are supposed to apply these protections without you filing anything. In practice, incorrect bills still go out. If you get billed for something on the "protected" list, that's typically a billing error worth disputing — see our guide on what to do if a doctor bill shows up after insurance already paid.

Before the appointment

How to confirm network status the right way

  1. Call member services, not the provider. Use the number on your insurance card. Provider directories are frequently outdated by months.
  2. Give the exact provider name, specialty, and location — network status can vary by office location even for the same doctor.
  3. Ask specifically about facility fees if you're scheduling anything at a hospital or surgical center — the facility and the doctor are billed separately.
  4. Request a reference number for the call and write down the date, time, and representative's name.
  5. Ask about ancillary providers — anesthesiologists, pathologists, radiologists — who you likely won't choose yourself but who will bill separately.
  6. If anything is out-of-network, ask about a network gap exception before the appointment, not after. If your insurer denies the claim afterward anyway, our guide to appealing an insurance denial covers the next move, and how long that review typically takes.
What to do right now
If you have an appointment coming up, call your insurer today and confirm network status in writing before you go — don't rely on the provider's website. If you're choosing a plan during open enrollment, check the network size for the doctors you already use before you compare premiums; a cheaper plan with a thin network can cost more overall. Frequently asked questions
If I go to the ER out-of-network, am I protected from a big bill?
In most cases, yes. Under the federal No Surprises Act, emergency care must be covered as if it were in-network, no matter which hospital you end up at. Your cost-sharing is calculated using in-network rates, and the provider generally cannot balance bill you for the difference.
My in-network doctor performed surgery at an out-of-network facility — what happens?
The No Surprises Act typically protects you when an out-of-network provider, like an anesthesiologist or assistant surgeon, treats you at an in-network facility without your knowledge. You should only owe your normal in-network cost-share for that provider. The facility itself may bill separately, so it's worth confirming the facility's network status in advance.
Can a provider still balance bill me if my plan has no out-of-network benefits at all?
Yes, outside of the situations the No Surprises Act covers. If your plan is an HMO or EPO with no out-of-network coverage, a non-emergency visit to an out-of-network provider can leave you responsible for the entire bill, not just a higher coinsurance share.
I got a referral to an out-of-network specialist — will it be covered at the in-network rate?
Not automatically. A referral from your in-network doctor doesn't change the specialist's network status. You typically need your insurer to approve a formal network gap exception in advance, in writing, before the visit counts at the in-network rate.
How do I check if a provider is in-network before my appointment, not after?
Call the member services number on your insurance card and ask the representative to confirm network status for that specific provider, location, and plan, then request a reference number for the call. Provider directories and search tools are frequently out of date.
