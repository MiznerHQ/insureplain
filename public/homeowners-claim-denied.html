Homeowners Claim Denied?
The Fork That Decides What's Next

Home Insurance

Written by the InsurePlain Editorial Team · Reviewed for accuracy · Last updated September 2026

⚠️ Educational only. InsurePlain does not provide insurance, legal, or financial advice. Always consult a qualified insurance professional about your specific situation.
What's actually happening

You filed a homeowners claim expecting your insurer to make you whole. Instead you got a letter — either an outright denial, or a check that's smaller than what repairs actually cost. Most advice online treats these as the same problem with the same fix: "appeal it, maybe hire a lawyer." They aren't the same problem.

Whether your insurer said "not covered" or said "covered, but here's less than it costs" determines which tool actually gets you paid. One path lets you force a fast, binding resolution without a lawyer. The other requires you to fight the coverage decision itself. Using the wrong one wastes the time you don't have — because both paths run against a real deadline.

Quick answers

What people ask most

Is a low settlement offer the same thing as a denial?
No — and the distinction matters. A low offer means your insurer agrees the loss is covered but disputes the dollar amount. That's an underpayment dispute, and it's the one situation where the appraisal clause applies.
Do I need a lawyer to invoke the appraisal clause?
No. Appraisal is designed to be used without an attorney. You write a demand letter, hire an independent appraiser, and the process runs on its own — though you can still consult a claims adjuster or attorney if the numbers are large.
What if my denial letter doesn't cite a specific policy provision?
That's a red flag worth escalating. Insurers are generally required to give a specific, policy-based reason for denial. A vague or missing citation is grounds for a pointed follow-up request and, if ignored, a state insurance department complaint.
Can I still appeal after invoking appraisal?
Appraisal and appeal serve different disputes, not different stages of the same one. If appraisal resolves the amount but you still believe part of the loss was wrongly excluded, that coverage question isn't settled by appraisal and can still be pursued separately.
The distinction that decides everything

Denial vs. underpayment: which one do you have?

Read your letter or settlement breakdown closely — the wording tells you which fork you're on. If you're unsure why the reason applies, the most common denial reasons are worth reviewing first.

  • Full denial (coverage dispute) — Your insurer says the cause of loss isn't covered at all: excluded peril, lapsed policy, late filing, or a pre-existing condition. Nothing gets paid until the coverage decision itself changes.
  • Underpayment (valuation dispute) — Your insurer agrees the damage is covered but the payout is lower than it actually costs to repair or replace, often due to a dispute over actual cash value vs. replacement cost, scope of damage, or material pricing.
  • Mixed claims — Common on larger losses: part of the claim is denied outright (say, water damage from "lack of maintenance") while the covered portion is separately underpaid. Each part needs its own path.
Your two tools

Appraisal clause vs. formal appeal

Nearly every standard homeowners policy contains an appraisal clause — a built-in, binding way to settle a dollar-amount dispute without going to court. It only works for one type of disagreement, though.

Appraisal Clause — For Underpayment

You and your insurer each hire an independent appraiser. If they disagree, the two appraisers select a neutral umpire. Any two of the three agreeing sets a final, binding payout amount — no lawyer required, no courtroom.

Formal Appeal — For Denial

Appraisal cannot rule on whether something is covered. For an outright denial you need a written formal appeal, and if that fails, a state insurance department complaint or an attorney to challenge the coverage decision itself.

⚠️ Both paths run against the same clock

Most homeowners policies include a suit-limitation clause requiring a lawsuit within one year of the loss, though many states legally require insurers to allow longer — sometimes two to six years. Check your policy's "Suit Against Us" section and confirm your state's rule before you assume you have time.

How to invoke the appraisal clause

The underpayment path, step by step

  1. Confirm the dispute is about amount, not coverage. If your insurer hasn't denied the peril itself, you're eligible for appraisal.
  2. Find the appraisal clause in your policy. It's usually under "Loss Settlement" or "Conditions" — the exact wording controls the process.
  3. Send a written demand for appraisal. Reference the clause by name and request your insurer name their appraiser.
  4. Hire your own independent appraiser. Not your claims adjuster — a separate, qualified appraiser working for you.
  5. Let the two appraisers select a neutral umpire if they can't agree on the amount themselves.
  6. Receive the binding decision. Any two of the three parties agreeing sets the final payout — your insurer is contractually bound to pay it.
What to do right now
Read your denial letter or settlement breakdown and identify which fork you're on before doing anything else. If it's a dollar-amount dispute, send your written appraisal demand now — it's faster and cheaper than fighting through appeals. If it's an outright denial, start your formal written appeal today and note the exact deadline in your policy's suit-limitation clause. Understanding how long the process typically takes can help you plan around that deadline realistically.
Frequently asked questions
What's the difference between a denied claim and an underpaid claim?
A denial means your insurer says the damage isn't covered at all — usually citing an excluded peril, a lapsed policy, or a pre-existing condition. An underpayment means they agree the damage is covered but the check they cut is less than it actually costs to repair or replace. These are different disputes with different tools: underpayment disputes usually go through the appraisal clause, while denials require challenging the coverage decision itself.
What is the appraisal clause in a homeowners policy?
The appraisal clause is a provision in nearly every standard homeowners policy that lets you force a binding, neutral resolution to a dispute over the dollar amount of a loss, without going to court or hiring an attorney. You and your insurer each hire an independent appraiser; if those two disagree, they select a neutral umpire, and any two of the three agreeing sets the final, binding payout.
Can I use the appraisal clause if my claim was denied outright?
Usually not. Appraisal only resolves disputes over the amount of a covered loss — it does not decide whether something is covered in the first place. If your insurer denied coverage entirely, you need to challenge that coverage decision through a formal appeal, a state insurance department complaint, or litigation, not appraisal.
How long do I have to sue my homeowners insurer?
Most homeowners policies include a suit-limitation clause requiring you to file a lawsuit within one year of the date of loss, though many states override this by law and require insurers to allow longer — in some states two to six years. Check your denial letter and your policy's "Suit Against Us" or "Legal Action Against Us" section, and confirm the actual rule for your state, since missing this deadline can permanently end your right to sue.
Does filing a state insurance complaint stop my deadline to sue?
Not automatically. A state insurance department complaint can pressure your insurer to reconsider, but in most states it does not pause or extend your contractual or statutory deadline to file a lawsuit. If your suit-limitation deadline is approaching, don't wait on a state complaint's outcome — get documentation moving and consider consulting an attorney about preserving your right to sue.
